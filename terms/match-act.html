MATCH Act

Export controls can restrict an item to a whole country or merely to named entities within it. The current regime on deep ultraviolet immersion lithography is the latter, which has left a gap: roughly ninety immersion systems a year flowed to China in 2024 and 2025, worth over $10 billion and close to two-thirds of one vendor's entire immersion exports. The MATCH Act would close that by making the control countrywide and extending it to servicing.

The stakes are set by what the tools enable. The exportable generation is sufficient for seven-nanometre logic and current high-bandwidth memory, which is what China's flagship indigenous AI chips use. Analysis behind the bill projects that stopping exports now caps indigenous production at roughly a tenth of United States output into the mid-2030s, while continuing them produces scenarios where the ranges overlap. The bill is bipartisan and bicameral and sits in the Senate version of the annual defence authorisation, so its fate turns on conference negotiation rather than on a standalone vote.

Deeper dive

How it works. The bill sorts semiconductor manufacturing equipment into buckets and elevates the most capable — immersion lithography plus a few other categories — to countrywide control, meaning the item cannot enter the country at all rather than being barred from named firms. Servicing is included, which matters because installed tools require vendor support to stay productive. Allied alignment is the mechanism's weak point: the tools are made in two allied countries, so a United States control is only as effective as their participation.

Why it matters now. Leverage over compute decreases monotonically from remotely accessed chips, through exported chips, to chips made domestically — and once a country can make its own, the chokepoint stops functioning as a governance tool. Lithography is the last tool in the chain China is expected to indigenise, with a commercial domestic system projected no earlier than the mid-2030s and current market share at zero, while etch, deposition and other segments are progressing. That makes the window for this particular lever finite and currently open.

Trade-offs. Countrywide controls forgo revenue for allied vendors and invite retaliation, with rare-earth restrictions the precedent; the argument advanced for gradual long-term restriction is precisely that it lowers that risk. The projections also rest on assumptions — that China indigenises the rest of the supply chain, and that import rates continue — which their authors mark as bounds rather than forecasts. And controls written against equipment are the mirror image of the approach the other side is taking, which writes thresholds against model parameters and training compute.

What to watch. Whether the provision survives defence-authorisation conference, whether Japan and the Netherlands align, whether the import rate changes in anticipation, and whether servicing restrictions prove more or less consequential than the export ban itself.

GPU access

GPU access is the question of who can obtain frontier compute and on what terms, from hyperscalers that lock up the supply pipeline to everyone outside it facing scarcity.

Compute as currency

Compute as currency is the view that once models become commodities, advantage is decided by access to compute and energy rather than by access to any model, and that compute is increasingly traded, for revenue shares or training tokens, rather than simply sold by the hour.

AI regulation

Government and quasi-government rules for AI: pre-release testing frameworks, audit and kill-switch requirements, incident disclosure, compute thresholds, copyright and chatbot rules, and the lobbying for and against them.

Open weights regulation risk

Open weights regulation risk is the possibility that government rules restrict, burden or effectively ban the release and use of open-weight AI models, either through explicit prohibition or through compliance obligations that only closed vendors can meet.
