Open model adoption metrics

first seen 2026-W32 · 4 links · 3 min read

Open model adoption metrics answer a simple question: are open-weight models being used, and by whom. The raw signals are download counts from model hubs, the number of derivative fine-tunes built on a base model, token volumes routed through public inference gateways, the share of requests inside open coding tools, and surveys of which models companies run in production. Because download counts favour older and smaller models, the more careful versions normalise for time since release and model size so that a new large model can be compared fairly with a small model that has been available for a year.

For an executive these metrics matter because they reveal where the market is moving before pricing catches up. Usage-share figures now show open models taking a rising portion of workloads, with the US share of open-model usage reported to have risen from 10 to 58 percent in a year. That shift affects vendor negotiations, hosting decisions and how much of an AI budget should be committed to closed-model contracts.

Deeper dive

What it measures. Adoption is measured through several imperfect proxies. Hub downloads are the most visible but reward models that have been public for longer and are small enough to fetch casually, so a relative adoption metric divides download counts by time available and adjusts for parameter count. Derivative counts, meaning the number of fine-tunes and merges built on a base checkpoint, measure how much a model has become a substrate for other work. Gateway token volumes show what developers pay to run, coding-tool traffic shows which models programmers pick when the choice is free, and scans of research papers show which models scientists evaluate against. Enterprise surveys close the loop with usage-share figures inside companies.

Why it matters now. The debate over Open weights models has been long on opinion and short on data. Dashboards built around these metrics now show a consistent picture: Chinese-origin families such as Qwen dominate derivative counts and downloads, Western labs have re-entered with permissively licensed releases, and open models carry a majority of gateway token volume at a deep discount to closed frontier pricing. That measurement underpins the Open-closed capability gap argument, because a model that is close to the frontier and widely adopted exerts price pressure that a merely competitive but unused model does not.

Trade-offs. Each proxy has known distortions. Downloads count bots, mirrors and CI pipelines. Gateway volumes reflect the customers of a few intermediaries, not enterprises hosting on their own clusters. Usage-share surveys depend on who answers. Metrics also drift as behaviour changes: when hubs mirror models to regional caches or when a coding tool sets a default, the numbers move for reasons unrelated to preference. A serious reader triangulates across several signals rather than trusting one chart.

What to watch. Whether the normalised metrics continue to show Western open releases gaining derivative share, whether enterprise usage-share surveys hold up as sample sizes grow, and whether regulators begin citing adoption data when weighing Open weights regulation risk. The metrics themselves are becoming a strategic asset, since whoever defines the dashboard shapes which models are seen as winning.

Related terms

concept

Open weights models

Open weights models are AI models whose trained parameters are published for anyone to download, run and modify, in contrast to closed models reachable only through a vendor's API.

needed by 11 · 36 linksconcept

Open-closed capability gap

The open-closed capability gap is the measured lag between the best openly downloadable model and the best closed frontier model, usually expressed as the number of months it takes open models to reach a given capability level.

Qwen

Qwen is Alibaba's family of open-weight language and multimodal models, the most downloaded on the main model hub and the most common base for derivative models and academic research.
