Price elasticity

first seen 2026-W33 · 4 links · 3 min read

Price elasticity measures how sensitive customers are to price. If a small price cut produces a large jump in usage, demand is elastic; if buyers keep paying whatever a product costs, it is inelastic. In AI the evidence points to a specific shape: corporate spending data shows that buyers do not pay a premium proportional to how capable a model is. A model that is twice as good on benchmarks does not command twice the price, because most tasks have fixed requirements and any model that clears the bar is good enough.

For an executive this reframes pricing and procurement. As intelligence per dollar rises, the tier that satisfies a given job keeps getting cheaper, so budgets should be built around routing each task to the cheapest adequate model rather than standardising on the best. For vendors it means the frontier is a marketing position more than a pricing position, and the profitable market is the middle. Falling prices have so far produced more usage, not less spending.

Also seen as demand elasticity

Deeper dive

How it works. Elasticity is the ratio of the percentage change in quantity demanded to the percentage change in price. For AI models the useful version is elasticity with respect to capability as well as price: how much more buyers will pay per unit of benchmark improvement. Spending data indicates that answer is low. The mechanism is that task requirements are close to fixed, so once a model satisfies them, extra capability has little marginal value to that buyer. As intelligence per dollar rises across all tiers, the satisfying tier moves down the price ladder, and demand pools there. The frontier retains buyers only for the minority of tasks where the best model is the only one that works.

Why it matters now. The pattern explains several observed shifts. Frontier model pricing has come under pressure from a price war among leading labs and from open models available at deep discounts. Smart model routing exists because elasticity is high across tiers: sending each request to the cheapest capable model captures the saving directly. Buyers increasingly measure Cost per task rather than cost per token, since that is the unit in which the elasticity actually operates. And the divergence between usage and revenue, discussed as Token share versus revenue share, is the market-level symptom of demand pooling in cheaper tiers while headline revenue clusters at the frontier.

Trade-offs. The Jevons pattern cuts both ways. Every fall in the cost of producing code has so far produced more software rather than fewer engineers, which suggests cheaper intelligence expands total demand and total spend. But for any single vendor, elastic demand means price cuts are matched quickly and margins compress toward the cost of compute. For buyers, optimising purely on the cheapest adequate tier risks underestimating quality differences that benchmarks do not capture, such as reliability on long tasks, and creates switching churn as tiers shift. Elasticity also differs by segment: consumer and developer demand is highly price-sensitive, while regulated enterprise workloads pay for assurance more than capability.

What to watch. Whether spending data continues to show flat willingness to pay for capability, how fast the satisfying tier migrates downward with each release cycle, and whether vendors find pricing structures, such as outcome-based or per-task fees, that recapture value that per-token pricing gives away.

Frontier model pricing

Frontier model pricing is the per-token list price of the top-tier models, anchored at ten dollars per million input tokens and fifty per million output for both Astra and Fable 5.1.

Cost per task

Cost per task is the dollar cost of getting a unit of work actually completed by an AI system, measured at a given quality level, rather than the price per token or the score on a benchmark.

Token share versus revenue share

Token share versus revenue share is the distinction between which models process the most tokens and which capture the most money, with open-weight models winning volume while a few closed labs collect most of the revenue.

Smart model routing

Smart model routing sends each request to the cheapest model that can handle it, reserving frontier models for the steps that need them and often running a small local model as the dispatcher.
