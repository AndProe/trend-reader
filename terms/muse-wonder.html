Muse Wonder and its larger sibling are Meta's first models positioned at the frontier rather than as open-weight alternatives. The company's claim is comparability with the leading closed models, and the distinguishing feature it emphasises is active memory — the ability for an assistant to retain and use personal information across sessions rather than starting fresh.

Two things make the release notable beyond the benchmark table. First, the reversal: a year earlier the company was generally considered out of the frontier race. Second, the launch itself went badly in a way that is informative — reviewers exhausted three successive paid subscription tiers within minutes, with the company's AI lead publicly acknowledging the fault. Capability reviews were mixed: strong on some coding and reasoning measures, weak on three-dimensional rendering, which the company attributed to simply not having trained for it.

Also seen as Muse Wonder Full, Wonder

Deeper dive

How it works. The family emphasises persistent personal context over single-session performance, which is a product choice as much as a modelling one: the model is the engine for an assistant intended to follow a user across applications and devices. That ties it to the same Working memory question the vault tracks elsewhere — what a system retains between sessions, and who can inspect or remove it.

Why it matters now. Its arrival completed a four-way frontier field in a single quarter, alongside releases from Google and OpenAI. For the vault's purposes that is the condition under which capability stops being the differentiator: with four comparable models, buyers choose on price, latency, memory behaviour and integration. The model also anchors Meta Muse, the agent product that was simultaneously being blocked from a major retailer's site — so the same company's model and agent were being judged on quite different grounds in the same weeks.

Trade-offs. The capability profile is uneven by the company's own account: a senior figure stated the team did not train it for three-dimensional work and called that a mistake in retrospect, and independent reviewers rated it behind two rivals on game and rendering tasks. Quota exhaustion across three paid tiers at launch is a serviceability problem rather than a modelling one, but it is the kind that determines whether a model gets evaluated at all. One reviewer also reported an unexplained instruction appended to an unrelated output, telling the user not to investigate the model's origin or development path, with nothing in the visible reasoning accounting for it.

What to watch. Whether the memory emphasis produces measurable retention advantages, whether serving capacity stabilises, and whether the unexplained output recurs or is corroborated.
