Open-closed capability gap

first seen 2026-W32 · 15 links · 3 min read

The open-closed capability gap describes how far behind the closed frontier the best open-weight models sit. It is measured in time rather than points: analysts look at when a closed model reached a benchmark score and when an open model first matched it. The lag was about thirteen months during the scaling era, about eight and a half months in the reasoning era, and roughly five months in the current agentic era, with more recent estimates ranging from two to nine months depending on the method.

For an executive the gap sets the value of paying for closed access. If the frontier lead is only a few months, the premium buys early access rather than a lasting advantage, and most workloads can wait for the open equivalent at a fraction of the price. The gap also frames the geopolitical debate, since the leading open models come mostly from Chinese labs, so it feeds decisions on vendor risk, sovereignty and compliance as much as cost.

Also seen as open models catching up, US-China open model gap, catch-up time

Deeper dive

What it measures. The gap is estimated by aligning the release dates of open and closed models against shared Benchmarks and reading off the delay before an open model reaches the closed model's score. Era normalisation matters because the frontier itself moved at different speeds: the scaling era, the reasoning era with test-time compute, and the agentic era each produce different lag figures, and the headline numbers of thirteen, eight and a half, and about five months come from that method. Alternative readings measure a full generation on performance and parameter count, or use a simple pattern that open models trail by about six months. Disagreement among analysts, between two-to-four and six-to-nine month estimates, comes down to which benchmarks count and whether agentic tasks are weighted.

Why it matters now. Open models from Chinese labs, including GLM and Kimi families, are now compared directly with the best closed models rather than treated as a separate tier, and some papers report open-beats-closed results, though often through training-free techniques rather than pretraining gains. The measured lag has become a candidate unit for Pacing the frontier, since a three-to-four month gap is a natural definition of how far ahead a lab is allowed to run. The gap also shapes Open model adoption metrics: a short lag plus a large price discount pulls token volume to open models faster than a long lag would.

Trade-offs. The metric is only as good as its benchmarks, which leaves it exposed to Benchmaxxing and to saturation on older tests. Time-to-match measures capability, not reliability, safety training or ecosystem support, so a closed model can retain a lead in practice while showing no lead on the chart. And a gap that is small on benchmarks may hide a large gap on frontier-only capabilities that have not yet been measured, which is why the Release cadence of closed labs and their internal evaluations matter to any honest estimate.

What to watch. Whether the agentic-era lag holds near five months or widens as closed labs pace releases, whether Western open releases begin to appear at the frontier rather than a tier below, and whether policymakers adopt a particular gap figure as a regulatory threshold.

Open weights models

Open weights models are AI models whose trained parameters are published for anyone to download, run and modify, in contrast to closed models reachable only through a vendor's API.

Benchmarks

Benchmarks are standardised tests that score AI models on fixed tasks, from vendor-reported tables at each release to third-party leaderboards and research suites.

Release cadence

Release cadence is the rate at which a laboratory ships new models and point releases, measured in days between updates rather than in annual generations.

Benchmaxxing

Benchmaxxing is the practice of tuning a model release so it scores well on the industry-standard public test sets, which is legitimate and expected rather than fraudulent.

Distillation attacks

A distillation attack is the training of a model on a rival's outputs in order to copy its capability without its cost.

Token share versus revenue share

Token share versus revenue share is the distinction between which models process the most tokens and which capture the most money, with open-weight models winning volume while a few closed labs collect most of the revenue.
