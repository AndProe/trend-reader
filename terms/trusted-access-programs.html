Trusted-access programs

first seen 2026-W35 · needed by 1 · 10 links · 3 min read

A trusted-access program gives a selected set of organisations access to a model, or a mode of a model, that the lab will not release to the public. Examples include a lab's proactive cyber defence programme for governments and enterprises, a staged partner programme around a cyber-focused model variant, and research previews restricted to selected labs and manufacturers. Partners are vetted, placed in categories and bound by usage conditions.

For an executive, the implication is that the most capable tools may not be on the price list. Access can depend on who the organisation is, what it will use the capability for and how it will report. This changes procurement into a qualification process and gives labs discretion over who competes with what. It also affects assurance: external evaluators have described receiving limited API access and time windows, with some evidence held by internal teams they could not see, so the independence of pre-deployment testing depends on the program's terms.

Also seen as trusted access, Fair Wind, Daybreak, trusted partners

Deeper dive

How it works. A lab identifies capabilities it judges too dangerous or sensitive for open release, typically offensive cyber tooling or frontier biology, and establishes a program with an application, vetting, staged categories of partner and usage conditions. Partners may receive a distinct model variant, a mode with fewer safeguards, or keys to models before production safeguards are in place. Programs known by names such as Fair Wind and Daybreak, and initiatives like Project Glasswing, follow this pattern. External evaluators are a special class: one pre-deployment evaluation described ten business days of API access, a questionnaire, a researcher interview and a second internal team with elevated access whose evidence the external authors could not see.

Why it matters now. Labs have concluded that some capability levels require this treatment. One lab's misuse report states that frontier biology capability should be restricted in exactly this way, and cyber-specialised model releases are shipping with partner programs rather than open endpoints. From the demand side the same phenomenon appears as Access rationing: the best models are allocated, not sold. Security is also a driver; evaluators hold nonpublic model access and unsafeguarded keys, which makes them targets and has prompted published security updates.

Trade-offs. Restricting access reduces misuse and concentrates it in accountable hands, but it creates gatekeepers. Labs decide which governments, companies and researchers get the strongest tools, a discretion with commercial and geopolitical weight. Independent evaluation suffers when evaluators see less than internal teams, which weakens the assurance the programs are meant to provide. Compliance costs and staged categories are a Guardrails tax that falls on smaller organisations, and defensive tooling withheld from most defenders leaves them exposed to attackers who obtain equivalent capability elsewhere.

What to watch. Whether governments formalise these programs into licensing regimes, how Frontier model access terms evolve for evaluators, and whether benchmarks such as CyberGym become the qualification tests for entry.

Access rationing

Access rationing is the practice of labs and governments deciding who may use the strongest AI models, through model withdrawals from distrusted platforms, gated frontier tiers, nationality screening and trusted-partner programmes.

Project Glasswing

Project Glasswing is Anthropic's trusted-partner programme that gives a vetted group of companies, including Visa, access to certain Mythos capabilities that are withheld from general release.

Frontier model access

Frontier model access is the question of which governments, allies and organisations are permitted to use the strongest AI models, treated increasingly as a national-security instrument rather than a commercial decision.

Guardrails tax

The guardrails tax is the claim that safety restrictions on a model reduce its capability, so that a less restricted sibling outperforms a more restricted one on the same tasks.

6 linksmodel

Mythos 5.1

Mythos 5.1 is Anthropic's restricted, higher-capability sibling of Claude Fable 5.1, offered only through trusted-access programmes and described as the same model without the safety classifiers that the public release carries.

7 linksproduct

CyberGym

CyberGym is a benchmark of real-world vulnerability discovery and exploitation tasks, with a public leaderboard that ranks how well AI agents find and exploit flaws in actual software.
