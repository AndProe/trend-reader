AI copyright

first seen 2026-W36 · 4 links · 3 min read

AI copyright has two halves. The input side asks whether training on copyrighted books, articles and recordings without a licence is fair use. The output side asks whether a model that reproduces lyrics, long passages, a character or a recognisable voice infringes, and what the lab must do to stop it. Both are being decided through litigation, settlements and lobbying rather than statute.

The main case is the New York Times suit against Microsoft and OpenAI, where the defendants argue fair use and the US government has sided with OpenAI. Authors and publishers are contesting settlement money, new newspaper suits keep arriving, and labs are lobbying Australia for conditional approvals and access to publicly available information. Labs respond in product: system prompts now forbid reproducing lyrics and long passages, and voice replication is withheld in several jurisdictions. For an executive this is a supplier-risk and indemnity question, and the market for insurance cover is thin because insurers cannot price it.

Also seen as fair use for AI training, copyright guardrails, training data copyright

Deeper dive

How it works. A model trained on a large corpus stores statistical regularities, not copies, but it can regenerate memorised passages, and it can imitate a style, a character or a voice on request. Plaintiffs argue that the copying happens at ingestion and again at output; defendants argue that training is transformative and that reproduction is an edge case they suppress. Suppression happens in the product layer: system-prompt prohibitions on lyrics, long verbatim passages and copyrighted characters, output filters, and geographic withholding of features such as voice replication where likeness rights are strongest. These guardrails are part of a model's Refusal behaviour and they arrive in step with litigation.

Why it matters now. The New York Times case against Microsoft and OpenAI is the test of the fair-use defence, and the US government's brief in support of OpenAI signals where federal policy leans. Settlements have created a pool of money that authors and publishers are now fighting over, and further newspaper suits have followed. Outside the US, labs are lobbying Australia for conditional approvals and a right to train on publicly available information. The unresolved liability shows up in insurance: buyers want cover for AI outputs and almost nobody is willing to write it, producing a lemons market. These decisions sit inside AI regulation more broadly and feed the AI data supply chain, because licensed data becomes more valuable as unlicensed data becomes riskier.

Trade-offs. Strict output guardrails degrade legitimate uses, such as quoting for criticism, and make refusals feel arbitrary. Licensing everything favours incumbents who can pay. A fair-use ruling for training would settle the input question but leave output liability with the deployer. Jurisdictional withholding fragments product features by country.

What to watch. The Times ruling and any appeal, whether settlements set a per-work price that becomes a de facto licence rate, whether Australia and the EU legislate a training exception, and whether an insurance market for AI output liability forms.
