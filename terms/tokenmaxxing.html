Tokenmaxxing

first seen 2026-W34 · 4 links · 3 min read

Tokenmaxxing describes what happens when a company measures AI adoption by consumption. Engineers learn that dashboards track tokens used or prompts sent, and they generate usage to look compliant: running agents on trivial tasks, letting sessions run long, or feeding the model work they would have done faster by hand. The term began as an early warning and is now quoted back as a grievance by engineers who resent being scored on it.

For an executive, the issue is measurement integrity. Adoption targets tied to usage volume will be met whether or not productivity improves, and the resulting data will overstate both value and cost. The remedy is to measure outcomes such as cycle time, defect rates and delivered scope, treat token spend as a cost line rather than a success indicator, and watch for cultural side effects: mandates that produce performative usage also produce fatigue and quiet disengagement.

Also seen as token maxxing, inflating AI usage scores

Deeper dive

How it works. Goodhart's law states that a measure ceases to be a good measure once it becomes a target. AI usage metrics are unusually easy to game because consumption is under the individual's control and costs them nothing directly. An engineer under a mandate can run a coding agent on files that do not need changing, ask for verbose explanations, or leave sessions running to accumulate tokens. Leaderboards that rank teams on usage amplify the behaviour, and because token spend is billed to the company, the gaming carries a real cost.

Why it matters now. Executive pressure to show AI adoption has run ahead of the ability to show AI outcomes, so many companies have defaulted to what is easy to count. The phenomenon was flagged early as a predictable consequence of mandates and has since been reported as a common engineer complaint. It sits inside Engineering culture as a symptom of top-down adoption, and alongside AI fatigue as a source of cynicism: engineers who are scored on consumption stop treating the tools as tools. It also distorts Cost per task analysis, because inflated usage makes real productivity gains look more expensive than they are.

Trade-offs. Some usage tracking is legitimate; leaders need to know whether licences are used and where agents are failing. The distinction is between diagnostic telemetry and performance targets. Outcome metrics are harder to attribute and slower to move, which is why consumption metrics are tempting. A related pattern, AI on the resume, shows the same incentive at the individual level: signalling AI fluency because it is rewarded, independent of results.

What to watch. Whether companies shift adoption reporting from usage to outcomes, whether finance teams begin auditing AI spend for low-value consumption, and whether vendors, who benefit from token growth, resist providing the outcome-level telemetry that would expose it.
