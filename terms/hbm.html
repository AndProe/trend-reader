HBM

first seen 2026-W33 · 5 links · 3 min read

HBM stacks several DRAM dies vertically and connects them to the accelerator through a very wide interface, giving far more bandwidth than ordinary memory modules. Every frontier GPU depends on it, and memory makers have shifted wafer capacity toward it: SK Hynix has committed on the order of $720 billion to sustained AI-driven memory demand. The side effect is a shortage of conventional DRAM and SSDs, with prices roughly quadrupling and a lobbying fight in Washington over production mandates tied to CHIPS Act funding.

For an executive the consequences arrive through several bills. Server memory for CPUs has become more expensive because HBM competes for the same wafers, which feeds the current CPU shortage. Edge and robotics products that rely on LPDDR are squeezed too. The design direction now favours bandwidth over capacity, which means future accelerators may carry less memory per chip rather than more. Procurement plans for anything memory-heavy should assume tight supply through the next generation.

Also seen as High Bandwidth Memory, memory chip shortage, HBM4

Deeper dive

How it works. HBM is DRAM stacked in layers, eight or twelve high in current parts, joined by through-silicon vias and placed on the same package as the accelerator. The short, wide connection delivers bandwidth in the terabytes per second, which is what a large model needs to read its weights and its KV cache fast enough to keep the compute units busy. Inference is dominated by data movement rather than arithmetic, so memory bandwidth, not FLOPs, is often the binding limit on tokens per second.

Why the shortage. Memory makers earn more per wafer on HBM than on commodity DRAM, so incremental wafer capacity has gone to HBM. Conventional DRAM and SSD supply tightened, prices quadrupled, and the squeeze reached server memory for CPUs, which is one reason CPU pricing and CPU availability have deteriorated. LPDDR parts used in robot and device brains are caught in the same competition, which raises Edge AI hardware costs. The whole sequence, from a demand signal at the accelerator to shortages several layers down the supply chain, is a textbook Bullwhip effect. The political layer is a fight over whether CHIPS Act-tied mandates should force production of conventional memory alongside HBM.

The design turn. Analysis of inference workloads argues that capacity beyond the active working set adds cost without revenue, and that bandwidth is the scarcer objective. That points to accelerators with fewer HBM layers per stack, reversing from 12-high to 8-high and potentially 4-high, with the freed budget spent on bandwidth. It also favours architectures that keep large tables in DRAM or SSD and stream them into HBM, where HBM bandwidth matters more than its size. The Vera Rubin generation and rack-scale designs such as the Jalapeno chip are where these choices become visible.

What to watch. Whether HBM4 supply ramps fast enough to loosen the accelerator pipeline, which bears directly on GPU access. Whether the 4-high forecast holds or capacity demand from longer contexts reverses it. And whether conventional memory prices fall back once new fabs come online or stay high as a structural feature of the AI build-out.
