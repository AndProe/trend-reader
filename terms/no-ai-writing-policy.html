No-AI writing policy

first seen 2026-W36 · 6 links · 3 min read

A no-AI writing policy is a stated editorial commitment. The publication declares that its text is human-written, that AI tools may be used to research, gather sources or check spelling, and that any research produced by AI is verified before use. A prominent engineering newsletter adopted this stance publicly on its fifth anniversary and applies the same rule to an essay competition it runs.

For an executive the policy is a signal about the market for trust. When generated text is cheap and abundant, provenance becomes a product feature: readers pay for the assurance that a person stands behind the words. Companies publishing thought leadership, documentation or customer communication face the same choice, and the decision has costs on both sides. Declaring human authorship raises production cost and constrains scale; not declaring it risks the content being discounted as filler. The policy also creates a compliance question for teams whose writers already use AI drafting tools quietly.

Also seen as human-written policy, no AI-generated text

Deeper dive

What it is. The policy draws a line through the writing process. Upstream of the draft, AI is allowed: searching, summarising sources, checking facts, with the requirement that the writer verifies anything the tool produced. The draft itself and every edit to it are human. Downstream, AI may check typos. The rule is enforced by declaration and by the publication's reputation rather than by any technical means, since AI text detection is unreliable and AI text watermarking only works when the generating model cooperates.

Why it matters now. The policy is a reaction to AI slop: the flood of competent, generic text that generated content has made cheap. A publication whose value is judgement and reporting cannot let its output be indistinguishable from that flood, so it makes its process the differentiator. The choice is also a bet about readers, that a meaningful segment will pay for verified human authorship, which the publication's continued growth supports. The same tension runs through Agentic journalism, where agents gather and draft and the open question is how much human authorship the audience requires.

Trade-offs. The policy forgoes real productivity. Drafting with AI and editing heavily is faster than writing from scratch, and the line between research and drafting is porous: a detailed AI-generated outline is arguably a draft. Enforcement depends on writers' honesty, and the policy cannot be proven to readers. It also risks a purity contest in which any AI assistance is stigmatised, which would push writers to hide rather than disclose their use. The counter-position, disclosure without prohibition, is more honest about practice but weaker as a brand.

What to watch. Whether other publications adopt similar policies or move to labelled disclosure, whether readers demonstrably reward human-only content, and whether standards bodies define what counts as AI-assisted versus AI-written.

AI text watermarking

Hidden statistical patterns embedded in model output at generation time so that a cooperating detector can later identify it as machine-produced.

Agentic journalism

Agentic journalism is news production in which agents monitor sources, research, draft and do much of the editing under rules written by a human, who keeps final judgement on only a subset of stories.

6 linksconcept

AI slop

Low-effort AI-generated content that costs the reader more than it cost the sender: pitches, pull requests, code shipped by founders who cannot maintain it, drive-by contributions to open-source projects, mass-produced marketing.
