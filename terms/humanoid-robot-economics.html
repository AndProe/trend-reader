Humanoid robot economics

first seen 2026-W35 · 6 links · 3 min read

A humanoid robot's cost is driven less by motors and more by its brain. The planning and vision models need accelerator-class compute, which can either ride on the robot or run in a data centre and stream instructions over a network. Analysis of total cost of ownership compares an onboard Jetson Thor with an offloaded B300 and finds that shared data centre GPUs cost less per unit of compute once about five robots share them, because a home robot is only active 4 to 8 percent of the time. Meanwhile Chinese makers claim aggressive deployment and unit prices, backed by industrial policy, while the training data needed for dexterity is expensive to collect.

For an executive evaluating robotics, the decisions are the make-or-rent choice for compute, the network and latency requirements that offloading imposes, and exposure to memory prices, since the LPDDR robot brains use is being squeezed by HBM demand. China's hardware lead is real; its unit economics on data collection are not yet solved.

Also seen as robot unit economics

Deeper dive

How the cost stacks up. A general-purpose humanoid needs a vision-language-action model for perception and planning and a fast controller for balance and manipulation. The controller must run locally, but the planner is the expensive part, and it can run on the robot or in a data centre. Onboard compute such as Nvidia's Jetson Thor costs money whether or not the robot is working, and it draws power from a battery that also has to move the body, which makes Performance per watt the governing metric for On-device inference in this setting.

The make-or-rent decision. The decisive term is utilisation. A home robot is estimated to be active 4 to 8 percent of the time, so its onboard accelerator idles most of the day. A data centre GPU such as a B300 can serve several robots in turn, and total-cost modelling finds shared data centre compute cheaper per FLOP once roughly five robots share it. That favours offloading the planner and keeping only reflexes on the robot, which is the same pattern as Edge AI generally: latency-critical work local, heavy reasoning remote. Factory robots with high utilisation can justify onboard compute; consumer robots mostly cannot.

Trade-offs. Offloading needs reliable low-latency connectivity and raises the cost of an outage from an idle robot to a stalled one. Onboard compute is private and resilient but pays for memory, and the LPDDR that robot brains use is being squeezed by HBM demand at the wafer level. Improving dexterity requires training data collected by teleoperation or real deployment, which is expensive and slow, and approaches such as Test-time training that let a robot adapt in the field add compute back onto the device. On the supply side, Chinese makers report deployment numbers and unit prices supported by industrial policy, so their hardware lead is real, while the Gross margin on a humanoid sold at consumer prices remains unproven.

What to watch. Whether robot-first factories reach scale and what dexterity level they require. Whether wireless offloading becomes standard in consumer designs. And whether memory prices ease enough to make onboard compute cheap again.

On-device inference

On-device inference means running a language model on the user's own hardware, whether a laptop, a phone, a robot or an on-premise server, instead of calling a cloud API.

Edge AI

Edge AI is the running of model inference on local hardware, from a desktop Mac or a single GPU to the processor inside a robot or a pair of headphones, rather than in a data centre.

Gross margin is revenue minus the direct cost of serving, expressed as a share of revenue; in AI it is the number that says whether selling tokens is a business.
