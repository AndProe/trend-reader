Influence operations

The practice predates generative models; what has changed is unit cost. Producing plausible, varied, locally specific text at volume used to require people, which bounded how many positions a network could hold and how convincingly it could hold them. That bound is gone, and the operational signature shifts from repetitive content to account-behaviour patterns.

The consequence for anyone reading sentiment data is direct. A platform's disclosure of a roughly 200,000-account network included 200 accounts working specifically to build American opposition to data centres — a tiny fraction of the network, disclosed while the remaining purposes were not. That single detail does not explain measured local opposition, which involves millions of nearby residents, but it does introduce an attribution problem into any polling or social-listening result on a contested infrastructure question. The honest position is that the measurement now carries an unquantified contamination term.

Also seen as coordinated inauthentic behaviour, bot farms

Deeper dive

How it works. A network is built from accounts acquired or aged over time, then directed at topics. Detection relies less on content analysis than on coordination signals — timing, provenance, device and network reuse, follower topology — because content quality is no longer a discriminator. Platform disclosures typically report a suspension count and a small number of illustrative purposes, which means the public learns the scale of a network and almost nothing about its distribution across topics.

Why it matters now. Data-centre siting has become politically contested, with polling showing voters within two kilometres of a facility swinging sharply against incumbents, and roughly 12 million people living inside that radius. Data center backlash is therefore a thread where real local grievance, legitimate advocacy and documented inauthentic activity coexist, and the vault holds evidence of all three for the same period. Separately, the same capability bears on elections and on the lobbying environment around AI regulation, where spending disclosures and apparent grassroots sentiment are both inputs to legislative judgement.

Trade-offs. Attribution is hard and over-attribution is its own failure: treating genuine opposition as manufactured is a way of dismissing it, and the disclosed 200 accounts cannot account for broad measured sentiment. Detection also sits in tension with AI text detection, which attacks the problem at the content layer where the signal has largely disappeared. Transparency reporting is voluntary and partial, so absence of disclosure is not evidence of absence.

What to watch. Whether platforms begin reporting topic distribution rather than only suspension counts, whether polling on contested infrastructure starts controlling for coordinated activity, and whether detection continues to migrate from content to behaviour as generated text becomes indistinguishable.
