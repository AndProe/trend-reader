Fine-tuning

Fine-tuning is the option most teams reach for and the one practitioners inside labs most often advise against. The reason is not that it fails but that it does not end: once a model's behaviour depends on your training run, every base-model upgrade becomes a migration, and the advantage you bought erodes as general models improve. The practical counsel that has emerged is to treat it as a commitment rather than a configuration — something you get married to for a while.

The economics have shifted, though, and in an interesting direction. Full-parameter fine-tuning is expensive and can interfere with capabilities the base model already had. Low-rank adapters change that calculation: a fraction of a percent of the parameter count, base weights frozen so nothing is overwritten, and adapters stored and routed from like a library. That turns fine-tuning from a one-time commitment into an accumulating asset, which is a different product than the thing people are usually advised against.

Also seen as finetuning, supervised fine-tuning, SFT, adapter tuning

Deeper dive

How it works. Full fine-tuning updates all weights on new data; parameter-efficient methods train a small added component while freezing the original, which bounds both cost and interference. The adapter variant has been measured at roughly 0.02 percent of a base model's parameter count per adapter, with base weights untouched — which is what makes a persistent library of many adapters feasible, each routed to the problems it was learned on. The data can come from outside or from the model's own failures, reflected on and distilled into an adapter immediately.

Why it matters now. Two developments pull in opposite directions. Context management improved to the point where long sessions hold more useful state without any training, which removes some of fine-tuning's historical justification. But adapter-based self-directed repair has produced measured gains of 8.9 to 17.0 percentage points in agent success rates with nearly doubled correction reliability and transfer across dozens of tasks — which is fine-tuning as Continual learning rather than as adaptation, and a genuinely new capability rather than a cheaper version of an old one.

Trade-offs. The maintenance obligation is real and the main reason for caution. Full fine-tuning risks capability interference. Adapters avoid that by construction but introduce a routing problem — a library you cannot select from accurately is a library you do not have — and raise a question nobody has answered: nothing in a self-directed loop evaluates whether the lesson written into the adapter was the right one. There is also an evaluation hazard: a fine-tuned model's gains are measured on the distribution it was tuned for, which is where they are least meaningful.

What to watch. Whether adapter libraries appear in production agent systems rather than papers, whether anyone publishes alignment evaluation of self-written adapters, and whether the standing advice against fine-tuning softens as the parameter-efficient variants mature.

Related terms

Post-training

Post-training is everything done to a model after its initial pretraining on raw text: supervised fine-tuning, preference optimisation and reinforcement learning on tasks and environments.

Self-distillation

Self-distillation is training a model on outputs produced by itself or by an earlier checkpoint of itself, rather than on data from a stronger teacher model.

9 linkstechnique

Continual learning

Continual learning is the ability of a model to keep learning from its deployment, updating on new tasks, users and information while retaining what it already knows, instead of being frozen at the end of training.

12 linkstechnique

Skill library

A skill library is a store of reusable, named procedures that an agent can load and apply, whether taught by demonstration, written by hand as instructions, derived from failure diagnoses or compiled into executable artefacts.

needed by 1 · 12 linkstechnique

Reinforcement learning environments

Reinforcement learning environments are synthesised, verifiable task settings, such as sandboxed code repositories, simulated devices or games, in which an agent acts, receives a graded reward and is trained on the result.

needed by 3 · 14 linkstechnique

Context management

Context management is the selection, compaction and hand-off of what a model sees across a long session.
