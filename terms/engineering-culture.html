Engineering culture

first seen 2026-W34 · needed by 10 · 17 links · 3 min read

Engineering culture is the unwritten operating system of a software organisation: how much autonomy teams have, whether experimentation and A/B testing are normal, how best practice spreads internally, and how much goodwill engineers extend to leadership. It is set from the VP of engineering and CTO seats, which is also where reorganisations under AI originate.

It matters now because AI adoption mandates are testing that goodwill. Engineers report frustration and eroding trust when productivity claims such as a threefold gain are asserted from above and experienced as a machine that never sleeps rather than as help. Where AI is imposed by mandate, the effects appear as fatigue, mercenary attitudes and quiet departures; where it is adopted by teams that keep their autonomy, the same tools raise output. For an executive the decision is how to introduce AI without spending the trust that makes engineering organisations work, and how to tell a productivity gain from a longer working day.

Also seen as Google engineering culture, engineering culture deep dives

Deeper dive

How it works. An engineering culture is the accumulated set of norms about who decides what, how work is reviewed, what is shared and what is tolerated. The strong versions run on developer goodwill, on internal channels that propagate best practice without management, and on the freedom to experiment, including A/B testing at a scale that lets teams learn from production rather than from opinion. These norms are shaped from the top: the VP of engineering and CTO roles are where engineering gets reorganised, and it is from those seats that AI mandates are now issued.

Why it matters now. The shift to AI-assisted development is being pushed harder and faster than earlier tooling changes, often with quantified productivity targets attached. Engineers describe frustration and eroding trust when a claimed threefold gain turns out to mean a computer that never sleeps and a working day that lengthens to supervise it. Mandated AI use, forced migrations with AI tools, and the reassignment of engineers to training work have all been cited as culture-breaking moves. The result is the pattern tracked under AI fatigue and Mercenary culture: engineers who once volunteered effort now do what is measured. Some teams have responded with a No-AI writing policy for certain documents, to keep human judgement legible.

Trade-offs. Autonomy and mandates conflict. Leaving adoption to teams preserves goodwill but produces uneven results and lets sceptics opt out; mandating it produces uniform tooling and uneven morale. Code review is the place the tension is most visible, since AI-generated volume swamps the reviewer capacity that culture relied on for teaching and trust. Executives who use the tools themselves, the Hands-on executives pattern, tend to issue more credible mandates because they know what the tools can and cannot do.

What to watch. Attrition among senior engineers at companies with explicit AI productivity targets, whether internal best-practice sharing survives when the practice changes monthly, and which cultures, the high-autonomy or the high-mandate ones, ship more over the next year.
