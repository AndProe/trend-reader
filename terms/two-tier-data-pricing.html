Two-tier data pricing

first seen 2026-W36 · 7 links · 3 min read

Two-tier data pricing offers one model under two contracts. On the standard tier the customer pays full price and the vendor never trains on their traffic. On the contributor tier the same model costs roughly 92 to 95 percent less, and in exchange the customer's prompts and outputs become training data. Meta introduced the pattern with Muse Spark 1.3, priced at $1.25 and $4.25 per million input and output tokens on the standard tier against $0.10 and $0.20 on the contributor tier.

For an executive, the spread is a price signal: the vendor values a customer's prompt data at roughly the difference, about $1.24 per million tokens, which at a billion tokens a day amounts to a privacy surcharge of around $454,000 a year. The decision is whether the organisation's prompts are worth more than that discount, which depends on what they contain, what regulation says and whether contributed data could strengthen a competitor's model.

Also seen as Standard tier / Contributor tier, contributor pricing, data-for-access barter

Deeper dive

How it works. The vendor prices privacy explicitly. The standard tier carries a no-training promise, the same guarantee that enterprises have negotiated as Zero Data Retention terms, but now sold at retail as a surcharge rather than granted by contract. The contributor tier waives that promise and discounts the model by around 92 to 95 percent. Because the model, latency and quality are identical, the price difference isolates the value of the data rights. In Meta's Muse Spark 1.3 pricing the spread is $1.24 per million tokens across the two tiers, which is the first publicly quoted, liquid price for prompt data.

Why it matters now. Labs need training data of the kind that only real usage produces, particularly for agentic and domain-specific tasks, and the AI data supply chain has not previously had a market price for it. A two-tier structure lets the lab buy data with discounts rather than cash, feeding a Data flywheel while price-sensitive customers subsidise it with their traffic. The pattern mirrors the advertising model, where users pay with data instead of money, and it vertically integrates the lab's product and training pipeline. The privacy surcharge, roughly $454,000 a year at a billion tokens a day, is small for large enterprises and large for startups, which sorts customers by how much their data matters to them.

Trade-offs. The contributor tier is attractive for low-sensitivity, high-volume workloads and dangerous for anything touching customer data, regulated content or proprietary know-how. Contributed prompts can encode business logic that later surfaces in a competitor's product. Consent flows through the customer, not the end users whose data may be in the prompts, which raises Enterprise data governance questions that a discount does not settle. A lab that leans on contributor traffic also concentrates on customers with the least sensitive data.

What to watch. Whether other labs adopt the structure, whether the spread narrows as data supply grows, and whether regulators treat training-consent discounts as a data transaction requiring disclosure.
