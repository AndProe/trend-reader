Hybrid bonding

Double a chip's width and length and you get four times the area for circuitry but only twice the edge for connections. That area-versus-perimeter mismatch is why modern accelerators are assemblies — processor dies surrounded by memory stacks — and why the speed of traffic between those pieces, not raw computation, often decides how fast an AI workload finishes.

Hybrid bonding attacks that limit directly. Earlier packaging put chips side by side and ran wires out the edges, then onto a shared silicon interposer, then bonded chips face to face using solder bumps that were bulky and resistive. Hybrid bonding removes the solder: align the copper interconnects of two chips, press and heat them so the copper expands and bridges, and the surrounding insulator bonds too. The result is far denser connections and lower energy per bit — which is why it is described as one of the technologies the industry is investing in most.

Also seen as direct bonding, copper-to-copper bonding

Deeper dive

How it works. The process aligns copper pads on two surfaces, then uses pressure and heat so the copper bridges the gap while the dielectric bonds. It comes in two forms with different economics: wafer-to-wafer, which dominates image sensors and requires dies that match exactly, with yield losses wherever a good die pairs with a bad one; and chip-to-wafer, where known-good singulated dies are bonded onto known-good base positions, so the yield is controllable. Practitioners expect both to persist, with chip-to-wafer favoured for logic-to-logic work.

Why it matters now. The pitch trajectory is the reason this is more than an incremental packaging improvement. First-level interconnect pitch has gone from 130-150 micrometres fifteen years ago to 25 micrometres in current high-volume production, with sub-10 in development and a 0.45-micron result published — roughly another order of magnitude of density beyond what ships today. Below about half a micron, the distinction between packaging and on-die wiring begins to dissolve: you can take layers off the top of a die and connect a lower internal layer directly to another die's lower layer, which is a different kind of device rather than a better-packaged one.

Trade-offs. It took years to reach volume because of alignment and defect challenges introduced by removing solder. Density gains also raise thermal questions, since stacking heat-producing logic has no obvious escape path — the limiter practitioners name alongside switching speed and switching energy. For non-AI uses the case rests on power rather than bandwidth: finer interconnects reduce picojoules per bit, cutting system power and heat for the same electrical performance.

What to watch. Whether published sub-micron pitches reach production, whether the packaging-to-interconnect boundary genuinely dissolves as practitioners expect, and whether thermal management becomes the binding constraint on stacking rather than bonding precision.
