Argon is Google's October 2026 frontier release, sold on deep work across multi-step professional tasks rather than on a single headline benchmark. It launched globally through Google's developer platform and to its paid consumer tiers, at an introductory two dollars per million input tokens and ten per million output, with cached input discounted by 95 percent, and with a one-million-token limit for long jobs.

Its significance is competitive rather than technical. The release left four companies holding frontier-class models at broadly similar capability and, in two cases, identical list prices — which is the condition under which buyers stop choosing on capability and start choosing on cost, latency and integration. The evidence Google offered was unusual: internal engineering outcomes, including a rise in agent-authored code submissions with fewer rollbacks, and specific optimisations contributed to public repositories, which are checkable in a way benchmark tables are not.

Also seen as Argon, Gemini 4

Deeper dive

How it works. Argon is served through Google's own accelerators, which matters because serving cost rather than model architecture is what the price reflects. Its positioning emphasises sustained multi-step work over single-turn answers, and reviewers noted it self-selects roughly half an hour of effort on an open-ended task unless told otherwise — so the effective Cost per task is partly set by the model's own default rather than by the buyer's configuration.

Why it matters now. The release landed in the same weeks as a cheaper near-flagship from one rival and a first frontier model from another, which turned a capability race into a pricing one. Independent cost analysis in the same period put five frontier models within five index points of each other at a 3.8x spread in cost per task, with this family cheapest — so the launch is best read as the serving-cost advantage of Custom silicon being converted into list price rather than retained as margin.

Trade-offs. Benchmark results are mixed rather than dominant: first place on some coding measures, fourth on the agentic terminal benchmark the vault tracks under Terminal-Bench, and near-parity on computer use. The introductory pricing is explicitly temporary. And the most persuasive evidence — upstream code contributions and internal submission-and-rollback figures — comes from the vendor's own engineering organisation, with no independent replication.

What to watch. Whether the introductory price holds, whether the rollback improvement is reproduced outside the vendor, and whether the agentic terminal gap closes in a point release.
