Project OT

first seen 2026-W35 · needed by 1 · 7 links · 3 min read

Project OT was an internal Meta initiative to reorganise engineering around AI. The premise was that with coding agents, teams could be 60 percent smaller and still deliver the same output, so the company planned to shrink teams accordingly. The plan was cancelled. The reported reason is that the productivity gains did not show up where it mattered: the volume of code produced rose, but the number of features shipped stayed flat.

For an executive the episode is a caution against sizing teams to a productivity assumption before the assumption is measured. Code volume is easy to count and easy to inflate; shipped outcomes are what the business pays for. The plan also had a human cost, contributing to an exodus of engineers who did not wait to see whether they would be among the 60 percent. Any AI-driven restructuring should be paired with outcome metrics and staged so that the organisation can stop if the gains fail to materialise.

Also seen as Organization Transformation, Meta's 60 percent team-cut plan

Deeper dive

What happened. Meta planned an Organization Transformation that would reduce engineering team sizes by around 60 percent, justified by the expected productivity of AI coding tools. The plan was detailed enough to circulate and to shape hiring and staffing decisions before it was withdrawn. When it was cancelled, the explanation that emerged was that AI had increased the amount of code being written without a corresponding increase in features delivered, so the arithmetic behind the cut did not hold. The episode overlapped with a broader Meta engineering exodus, as senior engineers left rather than wait for the outcome.

Why it matters. The plan is the largest known attempt to convert claimed AI productivity directly into headcount reduction at a top engineering organisation, and its failure is evidence in a debate that otherwise runs on vendor claims and anecdotes. It supports the view that current agents raise output at the level of code and pull requests while the bottlenecks, such as design, review, integration and deciding what to build, remain human and remain the same size. It also illustrates the Engineering as cost center framing, in which engineering is a cost to be minimised rather than a capability to be grown, and shows what happens when that framing meets an unproven productivity multiplier. Companies that call themselves AI-native companies are watching the same experiment from the other direction, trying to build small teams from the start rather than shrink large ones.

Trade-offs. The underlying question is not settled. Code volume up with features flat could mean that AI productivity is illusory, or that the organisation had not yet changed its review and release processes to absorb the extra output, or that the gain is real but arrives later than a single planning cycle. Cutting first and measuring later forecloses the ability to tell these apart, and it damages trust in a way that persists after the plan is withdrawn. The alternative, Org flattening by attrition and slower restructuring, is less dramatic but keeps the option to adjust.

What to watch. Whether Meta or others revive a version of the plan with outcome-based metrics, whether shipped-feature throughput at AI-heavy engineering organisations begins to rise, and how the exodus affects Meta's ability to execute on its AI strategy.

Meta engineering exodus

AI-native companies

Companies and teams built or rebuilt around AI from the start, typically very small groups supervising fleets of agents rather than large teams of individual contributors.

Engineering as cost center

Engineering as cost center is the management framing that treats software engineers as overhead to be minimised rather than as the people who generate the company's revenue.

Org flattening

Org flattening is the reduction of management layers and manager headcount in engineering organisations, a trend now three years running and accelerated by AI mandates and the reframing of engineering as a cost centre.
