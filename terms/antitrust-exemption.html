Antitrust exemption

first seen 2026-W38 · 9 links · 3 min read

Competition law treats agreements between rivals with suspicion, since coordination on prices, output or timing is how cartels work. The problem for AI is that several safety measures look like that. Two labs came close to a formal arrangement to test each other's models for dangerous behaviour, and the speculated obstacle was antitrust exposure. A coordinated slowdown, which some safety proposals require, would need the same protection, and one lab's legal review reportedly ruled it out without one. The competition regulator has refused such protection and called inter-lab agreement suspicious; a private lawsuit treats agreement between labs as restraint of trade; and one senator calls an exemption a reward for the labs.

For anyone in or around the industry, this is a live governance gap, not a technicality. Any collaborative safety initiative, from shared red-teaming to joint release standards, needs legal structuring that survives competition scrutiny, and the cost of getting it wrong is litigation. Policy engagement on a narrow, conditional safe harbour is the practical response.

Also seen as antitrust safe harbour, competition-law carve-out

Deeper dive

How it works. An exemption would define a class of inter-lab agreements that competition authorities agree not to treat as anticompetitive, typically under conditions: limited to safety purposes, transparent to the regulator, not extending to price or market allocation, and perhaps time-limited. Precedents exist in other industries where rivals share safety data or set standards under supervised arrangements. Without one, a lab's counsel must assume that any agreement affecting when or whether a product ships could be challenged as a restraint of trade, and the safest legal advice is not to agree at all. That is the mechanism by which the absence of an exemption produces less safety work: cross-testing deals stall, coordinated pauses are ruled out in legal review, and cooperation retreats to informal channels that carry no commitment.

Why it matters now. Several safety proposals in circulation depend on coordination. Pacing the frontier envisages labs deliberately restraining the pace of capability release, which is only stable if rivals do the same. Cross-lab Alignment evaluation and Embedded evaluators require labs to grant each other access that a competitor would normally be denied. The political environment has moved against the idea at the same time as the need has grown: the regulator refused protection, a private suit treats coordination as collusion, and the framing in Congress is that an exemption rewards the labs rather than constrains them.

Trade-offs. The objection is real. Frontier AI is a concentrated industry, and a safe harbour for coordination could become cover for Regulatory capture: agreeing to slow down together is hard to distinguish from agreeing to limit output, and the same labs would benefit from higher barriers to entry. A narrow exemption invites gaming at its edges; a broad one invites cartel behaviour. Conditions such as regulator oversight, public reporting and exclusion of commercial terms address this partly, but any design gives incumbents a forum that new entrants and open-weight developers lack. The alternative, regulation that imposes the same constraints on all labs from outside, avoids the collusion problem but is slower and politically harder.

What to watch. Whether the private suit over inter-lab agreement produces a ruling that clarifies the boundary. Whether any legislative proposal, including those emerging from the Congressional inquiry into the labs, includes a conditional safe harbour. And whether labs find structures, such as third-party evaluators acting as intermediaries, that achieve cross-testing without direct agreement.

Pacing the frontier

Pacing the frontier is the proposal that leading AI labs slow the release of their most capable models in a coordinated, verifiable way, for example by shipping every two months rather than continuously, rather than pausing altogether.

Embedded evaluators

Embedded evaluators are independent third-party staff placed inside a frontier lab with employee-like access to models, training runs and incidents, and the right to publish what they find.

Alignment evaluation

Alignment evaluation is the testing of whether a model behaves as its developers intend, covering deception, sabotage, reward hacking and other misbehaviour, as distinct from testing what it can do.
