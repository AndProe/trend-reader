P(doom)

first seen 2026-W37 · 8 links · 3 min read

P(doom) is shorthand for a probability estimate: how likely a given person thinks it is that AI leads to catastrophe on the scale of human extinction. It is a subjective judgement, not a measurement, and the number depends heavily on the time horizon and on what counts as doom. Estimates from staff at the leading labs span from close to zero to around 50 percent, and a prominent interpretability researcher has put the figure above 10 percent within a decade.

For an executive the number matters less than its distribution. When people building the systems assign double-digit probabilities to catastrophe, that belief drives their behaviour: it underpins proposals to pace the frontier, calls for enforceable safety standards, and the willingness of senior staff to resign or speak publicly. It also feeds political attention, from legislation aimed at banning superintelligence to editorials taking the risk seriously. P(doom) is a barometer of insider sentiment that affects regulation, talent and the licence to operate.

Also seen as extinction probability

Deeper dive

What it measures. P(doom) is a Bayesian credence expressed as a percentage. The person states how likely they consider a specified bad outcome over a specified period, and the value combines their beliefs about capability growth, the difficulty of alignment, the odds of deployment mistakes and the chance that institutions respond in time. Because each of those inputs is uncertain, estimates vary by orders of magnitude between individuals, and the same person's number moves with new evidence. Compilations of estimates from leading-lab staff show a spread from near zero to about 50 percent, and estimates above 10 percent within a decade are given by researchers working on interpretability, whose argument is that current tools cannot yet verify what models are doing internally.

Why it matters now. The debate moved from forums to mainstream politics in 2026. Insider estimates are cited in the argument for Pacing the frontier, where the case for coordinated slowdown rests partly on the claim that the people closest to the technology think the downside is material. A visible Preference cascade followed senior resignations, as previously private concern became public once a few people spoke. Legislative proposals such as the Ban Artificial Superintelligence Act draw on the same numbers, and so do critics who dismiss the risk as a hoax. In each case the probability is doing rhetorical work as well as analytical work.

Trade-offs. The metric has real weaknesses. It compresses many distinct scenarios into one number, ignores how actions taken now change the odds, and rewards confident-sounding figures over honest uncertainty. Numbers from people whose careers depend on AI going well, or on the perception that it is dangerous, carry incentives in both directions. A high P(doom) can justify concentration of power in a few labs as easily as it justifies caution. Yet the alternative, refusing to quantify, leaves decision-makers with no way to compare the seriousness of competing claims, which is why work on Alignment evaluation aims to replace intuition with measured evidence.

What to watch. Whether lab staff estimates converge or polarise as evaluation tools improve, whether any regulator adopts a probability threshold as a trigger for intervention, and how the numbers move after the next major capability jump or safety incident.

Related terms

concept

Preference cascade

A preference cascade is the moment when privately held views become public all at once after a few people speak first; in AI it names the September 2026 wave of lab staff admitting that advanced AI might kill everyone and that no lab has a plan to align superintelligence.

8 linksconcept

Recursive self-improvement

Recursive self-improvement is the use of AI systems to accelerate the research, data, code and infrastructure that produce the next AI system, so that each generation shortens the time to the one after it.

14 linksconcept

Pacing the frontier

Pacing the frontier is the proposal that leading AI labs slow the release of their most capable models in a coordinated, verifiable way, for example by shipping every two months rather than continuously, rather than pausing altogether.

needed by 1 · 16 linksconcept

Alignment evaluation

Alignment evaluation is the testing of whether a model behaves as its developers intend, covering deception, sabotage, reward hacking and other misbehaviour, as distinct from testing what it can do.

needed by 3 · 16 linksevent

Ban Artificial Superintelligence Act

The Ban Artificial Superintelligence Act is a bill introduced by Senator Bernie Sanders and Representative Greg Casar that would pause advanced AI development until safety standards exist, backed by a corporate death penalty, prison terms of up to 20 years and a new cabinet-level agency.

6 linksconcept

Lossy self-improvement

Lossy self-improvement is the thesis that AI does accelerate AI research, but that the gains leak away rather than compounding into a runaway, because automatable research is narrow, parallel agents show diminishing returns, and compute, capital and politics govern what gets built.
