Revenue-share licenses

first seen 2026-W31 · 7 links · 3 min read

An open-weight model can be downloaded and run by anyone, which raises the question of how its developer earns anything. The revenue-share licence answers with a threshold: below it the model is free to use, above it the deployer must sign a commercial agreement. Kimi K3 set the pattern at twenty million dollars of annual revenue, GLM-5.3 attached a security-review clause at ten billion dollars, and MiniMax M3 followed the same shape. The phrase used for the model is open until you scale.

For an executive this is freemium applied between a lab and the companies that build on its models. It preserves the adoption advantages of open weights, developer goodwill, ecosystem tooling, sovereign-deployment eligibility, while giving the lab a claim on the largest customers. Any organisation building on Chinese open models should read the threshold clauses now, because the point at which a free dependency becomes a negotiated one is defined by the customer's own revenue, not by anything the lab does.

Also seen as revenue-triggered licensing, open until you scale, commercial-threshold licenses

Deeper dive

How it works. The licence grants ordinary open-weight rights, including modification and redistribution, and adds a conditional clause: once a deployer's revenue exceeds a threshold, continued commercial use requires a separate agreement. The terms vary. Kimi K3 requires a commercial agreement at twenty million dollars a year; GLM-5.3 requires a security review at ten billion dollars; other licences add revenue-share provisions. Below the threshold the model behaves as any Open weights models release does, which is what keeps it inside the open ecosystem of hosting providers, fine-tuning tools and benchmarks.

Why it matters now. The threshold licence resolves a tension that has shaped the open-model debate. Chinese labs releasing frontier-class open models such as Kimi K3 and Qwen have used openness to win adoption and to commoditise the model layer beneath their competitors, an instance of Commoditise your complement. But a lab that gives its models away has no revenue from the largest deployers, who are exactly the customers able to pay. The threshold turns the open release into Product-led growth for the lab: free at the bottom of the funnel, priced at the top. It also carries policy exposure, since a commercial-agreement requirement on a Chinese model gives governments a contractual hook when they assess Sovereign AI models built on it.

Trade-offs. The mechanism only captures value if large deployers comply, and enforcement against a company that has already fine-tuned and internalised the weights is untested. Thresholds set in revenue rather than usage penalise high-revenue, low-AI businesses and spare AI-heavy startups until they scale. A security-review clause is an unusual condition for an open licence and blurs the line between licence and export control. And each new clause pushes the model further from the community definition of open source, which matters for eligibility in public procurement and for the lab's reputation among developers.

What to watch. Whether any threshold is enforced against a large deployer, whether Western labs adopt the same shape for their own open releases, and whether governments treat the review clauses as a reason to prefer or to avoid these models.
