Artificial Analysis index

first seen 2026-W34 · 7 links · 3 min read

The index is an independent scoreboard that runs a fixed set of evaluations against each released model and publishes a combined score, a ranking and a cost-per-index-point figure, with separate indexes for speech-to-speech quality and transcription accuracy. Because no lab produces it, it is the number cited when a vendor's own table is disputed: a five-point jump into first place, a fifth place at 46, or an open model with 18 billion active parameters matching a much larger closed one. Labs cite it for their own latency and error-rate claims.

It is the fastest way to compare models on a common basis, with the caveats of any composite. It measures a harness and prompting setup that may not match yours, its component benchmarks have grading errors that audits have revised, it was once rebuilt after an implausible score, and experienced users often find it disagrees with how a model performs on their work. Use it to shortlist and track price-performance, not as the final word.

Also seen as Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index, AA index

Deeper dive

What it measures. The intelligence index combines results from a standard set of public evaluations, covering reasoning, coding, mathematics and knowledge, into one number, and ranks models by it. A companion cost figure relates that score to the price of running the model, giving a cost-per-index-point that makes price-performance comparable across vendors. Separate indexes cover speech-to-speech quality, transcription word error rate and latency, and labs now cite those third-party numbers in their own release posts rather than relying on internal measurements. The index sits inside the broader Benchmarks ecosystem as the most-used aggregator of them.

Why it matters now. Every frontier release opens with a benchmark table, and the tables are hard to compare because each vendor chooses its own set and settings. The index supplies a common ranking, which is why reviewers reproduce it, analysts use it to compare open and closed models, and the Open-closed capability gap is often stated in its points. It also feeds the Cost per task argument: a model's position on the cost-versus-score plot has become more decisive for buyers than its rank alone, and small open models that match larger ones at a fraction of the cost show up clearly on that plot.

Trade-offs. Three limits recur. Harness dependence: the index runs each model in a particular scaffold, and results move with the scaffold, so a model tuned to a different harness may score below its practical ability. Component quality: an expert re-grading of physics questions found broken evaluations and revised reported scores upward, which means the composite inherits the flaws of its parts. Gaming: labs can tune releases toward industry-standard test sets, the practice of Benchmaxxing, and the index cannot distinguish tuned from general capability. The index was rebuilt mid-week after an implausible score for a new model, which illustrates both its responsiveness and its fragility. And it measures a different thing from job-relevant evaluations such as GDPval, so a high index rank does not settle whether a model does useful work.

What to watch. Whether the index adopts fixed, auditable harnesses so scores are reproducible. Whether cost-per-point becomes the headline figure rather than rank. And whether task-based evaluations displace composite indexes in procurement decisions, which would reduce the index to a screening tool.

Cost per task

Cost per task is the dollar cost of getting a unit of work actually completed by an AI system, measured at a given quality level, rather than the price per token or the score on a benchmark.

Benchmaxxing

Benchmaxxing is the practice of tuning a model release so it scores well on the industry-standard public test sets, which is legitimate and expected rather than fraudulent.

GDPval

GDPval is OpenAI's benchmark for real-world, computer-based knowledge work, reported as an Elo score that ranks models by how often their output is preferred on economically valuable tasks.
