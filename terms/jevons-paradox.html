Jevons paradox

The paradox, named for a nineteenth-century observation about coal and steam engines, is that efficiency can raise total consumption: when each unit of work gets cheaper, more work becomes worth doing, and the new demand can exceed the saving.

Applied to AI it is the argument that cheaper inference does not reduce compute demand. The vault records the pattern in the numbers: token prices fell 41 percent in a quarter while usage rose 50 percent, and the people making the efficiency case expect the effective cost of model intelligence to keep declining near-exponentially while demand rises, on the view that the industry is bottlenecked on how to deliver agents rather than on capability. For anyone planning capacity or budgets, the implication is that efficiency improvements should be modelled as demand stimulus, not as savings.

Also seen as Jevons effect

Deeper dive

How it works. Efficiency lowers the price per unit of useful output. Where demand is elastic — where there are many valuable tasks not currently worth their cost — the volume response can be larger than the price fall, so total spend rises. Agentic workloads are the elastic case: a single agent run consumes orders of magnitude more tokens than a chat turn, and work that was uneconomic at one price becomes routine at a fifth of it, which is why Agentic inference workloads grow faster than price declines reduce them.

Why it matters now. Two opposing readings are live. One holds that inference efficiency compounds toward the hardware's limits, with companies already saving 10 to 30 percent of serving cost after launch pricing, and that this drives diffusion rather than consolidation. The other observes that aggregate dollar spend among the heaviest adopters has cooled even as volume compounds, which is the divergence the vault tracks under Token share versus revenue share. Both can be true at once: more tokens, cheaper tokens, flat or falling revenue per customer — which is precisely the commoditisation condition.

Trade-offs. The paradox is not a law; it holds only where demand is elastic and complementary inputs are available. Here they are not unlimited: the vault records CPU and GPU scarcity, regions closed to new tenants, a skilled-trades shortfall of 45 workers per 100 needed, and chip executives naming financing cost as a tighter constraint than silicon or power. Efficiency gains that cannot be absorbed by capacity do not become demand.

What to watch. Whether volume growth continues to outrun price declines, and whether any of the physical or financing constraints binds first.

Related terms

metric

Compute efficiency

Compute efficiency is the amount of useful capability delivered per unit of compute, combining gains on the model side, such as fewer active parameters and cheaper inference, with gains on the chip side.

needed by 1 · 10 linksconcept

Agentic inference workloads

Agentic inference workloads are the production traffic pattern created by coding and computer-use agents: many turns per task, context that accumulates toward a million tokens, very high reuse of shared prefixes, and bursts of parallel sub-agent calls.

needed by 1 · 18 linksmetric

Frontier model pricing

Frontier model pricing is the per-token list price of the top-tier models, anchored at ten dollars per million input tokens and fifty per million output for both Astra and Fable 5.1.

needed by 2 · 24 linksconcept

Inference-time compute

Inference-time compute is computation spent when a model answers, through longer reasoning, multiple candidate answers, verification or tool calls, rather than during training.

needed by 4 · 16 linksconcept

Token share versus revenue share

Token share versus revenue share is the distinction between which models process the most tokens and which capture the most money, with open-weight models winning volume while a few closed labs collect most of the revenue.

10 linksmetric

Token efficiency

Token efficiency is the amount of useful capability a model delivers per token consumed or produced, whether measured as output tokens per task, accuracy per token, or the input-to-output ratio of a workload.
