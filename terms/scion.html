Version control is the least glamorous and most universal piece of software engineering infrastructure, and replacing it is something organisations avoid. The reported reason for doing so here is throughput: daily merged changes rising from roughly 2,500 in May to more than 30,000 in September, a volume at which Git's performance characteristics became the constraint. The replacement was built by a small team in about five months.

For anyone assessing what agentic engineering actually changes, this is a more informative datum than a benchmark score. It is a measurement taken from the tooling layer rather than from the model: the claim is not that code is better or that engineers are faster, but that the rate of merged change grew by roughly an order of magnitude in four months and the infrastructure underneath it had to be rebuilt. The second-order question — whether other organisations hit the same wall or whether this one is unusual — is unresolved, and the system has not been open sourced.

Deeper dive

How it works. The specifics are not public. The reported motivation is performance at high merge volume, which in Git terms points at the costs that scale with history size and concurrent branch count rather than at the data model itself. The relevant property for the vault is not the design but the threshold: a tool that serves essentially every software organisation stopped serving one of them at roughly 30,000 merges a day.

Why it matters now. The figure is the clearest available quantification of what Agentic engineering does to a repository, and it sits beside softer measurements that point the same way — a reported near-tenfold rise in code output per researcher at the same organisation, and external survey data showing merged pull requests roughly doubling year on year across a broad developer population. The pattern those share is that volume rises faster than anything else, which makes throughput-sensitive infrastructure the first thing to break.

Trade-offs. Building version control in-house trades a universal, externally maintained tool for one that must be maintained forever by the team that wrote it, and it isolates the organisation from the surrounding ecosystem of integrations. The decision not to open source it keeps that cost internal and also prevents the claim from being tested elsewhere. The merge-rate figure is self-reported and has no published denominator — engineers, services or repositories — so it supports a claim about direction more than about magnitude.

What to watch. Whether other organisations report the same ceiling, whether the merge-volume figure is ever normalised per engineer, and whether code review and CI — the next throughput-sensitive layers — are reported as the following constraints.

Agentic engineering

Agentic engineering is the discipline of building software with coding agents deliberately: engineers specify, constrain, verify and review while agents write most or all of the code, in contrast to unstructured vibe coding.
