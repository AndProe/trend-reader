AI text detection

first seen 2026-W34 · 4 links · 3 min read

AI text detection tries to answer a question many institutions want settled: was this written by a person? Detectors score text on features such as predictability and phrasing and report a probability. Commercial services publish feed-level estimates of how much online writing is machine-generated, and one prominent demonstration of a new model in September was its use as a detector, complete with percentages and a breakdown but no stated validation.

The problem is that the tools are unreliable in both directions. They flag human prose, which has produced complaints from writers accused of using AI, and they miss edited machine output. As people absorb AI phrasing and models are tuned to sound human, the two distributions converge and the signal the detectors rely on weakens. For an executive the practical rule is not to make consequential decisions, about hiring, discipline or publication, on a detector's score alone, and to prefer provenance mechanisms where they exist.

Also seen as AI detectors, AI-writing detection

Deeper dive

How it works. Classifiers exploit the fact that model output tends to be more predictable than human writing: each word is close to what a language model would itself have chosen, sentence lengths vary less, and certain constructions recur. A detector measures these properties, sometimes by scoring the text with a reference model, and thresholds the result. Some tools add stylistic features and training on labelled corpora. None has access to ground truth about how a text was produced, so every score is an inference from surface statistics, and light human editing, paraphrasing or prompting for a distinctive voice shifts a text across the boundary.

Why it matters now. Demand is rising with the volume of AI slop in inboxes, review queues and feeds, and some organisations have adopted a No-AI writing policy they would like to enforce. Feed-level measurements claiming large machine-written shares circulate widely and draw objections from writers who find their own work flagged. Meanwhile the underlying race is structural: people who read machine prose all day start to write like it, and models tuned for naturalness write like people, so the gap the detectors measure narrows from both sides. The same convergence appears in AI design homogeneity, where it is the sameness rather than the origin that gives the game away.

Trade-offs. A detector tuned to catch more machine text accuses more humans; tuned to protect humans, it misses more machine text. The costs are asymmetric and fall on the accused, who cannot prove a negative. Detection also does not answer the question institutions care about, which is whether the work is any good and whether the person understands it. The alternative is provenance at the source, through AI text watermarking, which works only where the producing model cooperates.

What to watch. Whether detection vendors publish validation on adversarial and edited text, whether institutions retreat from detector-based enforcement after wrongful accusations, and whether watermarking or content credentials displace statistical detection.

Related terms

concept

AI slop

Low-effort AI-generated content that costs the reader more than it cost the sender: pitches, pull requests, code shipped by founders who cannot maintain it, drive-by contributions to open-source projects, mass-produced marketing.

8 linksconcept

No-AI writing policy

A no-AI writing policy is a publication's rule that every published sentence is written by a person, with AI permitted only for research, fact-finding and typo checks.

6 linksconcept

AI design homogeneity

The tendency of AI-generated software, interfaces and visual output to converge on the same default look, layouts and choices, so that AI-built products become recognisable by their sameness.

3 linkstechnique

AI text watermarking

Hidden statistical patterns embedded in model output at generation time so that a cooperating detector can later identify it as machine-produced.
