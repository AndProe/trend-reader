Sol is the cheap sibling of OpenAI's flagship: two dollars per million input tokens and ten per million output against the flagship's ten and fifty, with cached input at ten cents, and reported at or near the flagship's level on the coding benchmark practitioners weight most heavily. It is slightly behind at the very top of computer-use performance.

The pattern it belongs to is the important part. Both leading labs shipped a large flagship and then a smaller model of nearly equal quality within weeks of each other, which suggests the step of compressing a frontier model into something cheaper to serve has become reliable. For buyers this decouples two things that used to move together: the price of a given level of capability is falling steeply, while the price of the absolute frontier — for speed or for the last few points of quality — is rising. Procurement decisions should be made against the first curve and budgeted against the second.

Also seen as GPT-6 Sol, Sol, GPT6.1 Soul

Deeper dive

How it works. A flagship is trained at full scale and then compressed, through distillation or further post-training, into a model that is cheaper to serve while retaining most of its behaviour. The economics follow from serving rather than training: a smaller model needs less memory bandwidth per token, so it is faster and cheaper per request, which is why the price can fall by four-fifths without a proportional capability loss. This is the mechanism behind the Post-training step becoming the commercially decisive one.

Why it matters now. Reviewers reported Sol at or above the flagship on a developer-sentiment coding benchmark at a fifth of the price, and the same month a competitor's cheaper sibling was described the same way. That is the supply side of the token-share shift the vault records: frontier models' share of tokens fell as buyers defaulted to cheaper tiers, and models like this are why defaulting down no longer costs much capability. It is also what makes Frontier model pricing comparisons unstable, since the relevant price depends on which tier a workload actually needs.

Trade-offs. The gap is real at the extremes — top-end computer use and the hardest reasoning still favour the flagship — so routing matters, and the vault's material on Cost per task shows the comparison only holds at a stated context size and effort level. Quality parity claims also come from vendor charts and practitioner sentiment rather than independent measurement.

What to watch. Whether the cheap tier's quality parity survives independent testing, and whether the flagship retains any workload other than speed-critical and frontier-hard tasks.

Post-training

Post-training is everything done to a model after its initial pretraining on raw text: supervised fine-tuning, preference optimisation and reinforcement learning on tasks and environments.

Frontier model pricing

Frontier model pricing is the per-token list price of the top-tier models, anchored at ten dollars per million input tokens and fifty per million output for both Astra and Fable 5.1.

Cost per task

Cost per task is the dollar cost of getting a unit of work actually completed by an AI system, measured at a given quality level, rather than the price per token or the score on a benchmark.

Small language models

Small language models are models designed to be small from the start, typically from around one to fourteen billion parameters, rather than large models compressed after training.

Token efficiency

Token efficiency is the amount of useful capability a model delivers per token consumed or produced, whether measured as output tokens per task, accuracy per token, or the input-to-output ratio of a workload.

Frontier models

Frontier models are the strongest AI models available at any moment, the tier that sets the state of the art and that everything else is measured against.
