Closed contributions

first seen 2026-W34 · 4 links · 3 min read

Open-source projects have traditionally accepted code changes from anyone willing to submit them, subject to review. Coding agents changed the arithmetic: a contributor can now generate a plausible-looking pull request in minutes without understanding the project, and popular repositories receive them by the thousand. Reviewing each one costs a maintainer more than the contribution is worth, so a small number of high-profile AI projects have adopted a blunt rule of not accepting external pull requests by policy, while others restrict AI-written submissions. Three projects have done so at the time of writing, and the expectation is that the practice spreads. The Linux kernel, by contrast, allows AI-written contributions.

For an executive who depends on open-source software, the shift matters in two ways. Projects that close contributions become more like vendor products with a single maintaining organisation, which changes the risk of relying on them. And any company running its own open repositories faces the same choice about what to accept.

Also seen as PRs not welcome, AI contributions policy, no external PRs

Deeper dive

What is happening. The pull-request model assumed that contributions were scarce and reviewer time was the cheaper resource. Agents inverted that. A repository that once received a few dozen pull requests a month can now receive thousands, many generated with little context, and a maintainer must read each to decide whether it is worth merging. Several top AI open-source projects have responded by declaring that external pull requests are not welcome: contributions come only from the core team, and outsiders are limited to issues and discussion. Others have taken the narrower step of banning or labelling AI-written submissions. Linux allows them, which shows the norm is unsettled.

Why it matters now. The practice is the open-source face of AI slop: content that is cheap to produce, superficially competent and expensive to evaluate. It puts a maintainer's Code review burden at the centre of the argument, since the cost of AI contributions is borne entirely in review. It also connects to the reliability of the platform itself; GitHub reliability has become a concern as volume rises. The trend cuts against the idea of a Software factory in which agents contribute freely to shared codebases; the projects closing contributions are saying that unrestricted agent output is a burden, not a resource.

Trade-offs. Closing contributions protects maintainers' time and the codebase's coherence, but it narrows the pool of ideas and fixes, weakens the community that open source depends on for succession, and blurs the line between an open project and a company's product with public source. Restricting only AI-written contributions is more targeted but hard to enforce, since provenance is difficult to verify. Automated triage, stricter templates and staged review are middle paths that trade tooling investment for openness.

What to watch. Whether the count moves beyond three projects, whether large foundations issue guidance, whether platforms add provenance or reputation signals to filter machine-generated submissions, and whether the practice prompts companies to bring critical dependencies in-house.
