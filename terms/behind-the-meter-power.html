Behind-the-meter power

first seen 2026-W33 · 9 links · 3 min read

A data centre normally buys power from the grid through a utility connection, which requires an interconnection agreement, upgrades to transmission, and participation in regional capacity markets. Behind-the-meter power puts generation on the developer's own side of the meter: gas turbines, reciprocating engines, fuel cells or renewables with storage, sized to run the facility with the grid as backup or not at all. Around 75 gigawatts of such projects have been ordered, and each must pass through several gates, from equipment delivery to permitting and fuel supply, before first power.

For an executive planning AI capacity the term marks a structural shift in where the constraint sits. Interconnection queues run for years and capacity auctions have produced high prices, so developers with capital and urgency are building their own generation. That makes them untouched by state-level pauses on grid connections, and it means ratepayer-funded auctions may find no counterparties. The trade is speed and control against capital cost, fuel risk, emissions exposure and the engineering complexity of running a power plant alongside a data centre.

Also seen as BTM power, onsite generation, off-grid datacentres

Deeper dive

How it works. A behind-the-meter project pairs a data centre with dedicated generation on the same site, connected directly rather than through the utility's meter. Gas turbines and reciprocating engines dominate the order book because they can be delivered and permitted faster than new transmission, with fuel cells and solar-plus-storage in supporting roles. Six gates stand between an order and first power: equipment lead times, site and air permits, fuel supply and pipeline capacity, water, skilled labour for construction, and the commissioning of generation and load together. A project may keep a grid connection for backup or run fully islanded, which removes the interconnection dependency entirely but forfeits the grid as a reserve.

Why it matters now. The argument that power, not money, is the constraint on AI build-out rests on the gap between capital available and megawatts deliverable. Grid interconnection queues run for years, and regional capacity auctions have produced prices that a modelling error once turned into billions of ratepayer cost, which is why proposals to repeat such auctions may find no data-centre counterparties. State-level moratoriums on new grid connections, such as the Texas pause, do not touch projects that never need the grid, so the effect of such pauses on the national build-out is smaller than headlines suggest. For Hyperscalers and the largest developers, on-site generation has become the default path for the biggest campuses.

Trade-offs. Speed comes at a price. On-site gas generation is more expensive per megawatt-hour than grid power in most regions, exposes the operator to fuel-price and pipeline risk, and carries emissions that draw local opposition, feeding the wider Data center backlash. Turbine lead times have lengthened as orders piled up, so the speed advantage narrows as more developers take the same route. Islanded operation removes the grid's reserve, so reliability engineering falls entirely on the operator. And the practice removes large loads from the public system, which affects Electricity prices for everyone else in ways that are argued in both directions: less demand on the grid, but also less contribution to shared infrastructure.

What to watch. How much of the 75 gigawatts on order actually reaches first power and on what timeline. Whether permitting for on-site gas tightens as emissions and local opposition mount. And whether Performance per watt gains in hardware reduce the need for new generation faster than developers can build it.

Orbital data centers

Orbital data centers are proposals to place AI compute on satellites, where solar power is continuous and cooling can radiate to space, with launch capacity rather than capital as the binding constraint.

Grid interconnection is the process, run through a grid operator's queue, by which a data centre or power plant is connected to the utility grid.
