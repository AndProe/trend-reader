SpaceX AI revenue

first seen 2026-W32 · 3 links · 3 min read

SpaceX has said its AI revenue could exceed its launch and satellite business by the fourth quarter of 2026. The path modelled for it is capacity: ten gigawatts of compute by 2027, sold at thirty to fifty million dollars per megawatt-year, which works out to roughly three hundred billion dollars of annual recurring revenue, with Microsoft projected as the largest buyer. The claim rests on building power and data-centre capacity faster than incumbents, including in orbit.

For an executive the figure is best read as a scenario rather than a forecast, since the evidence for it is a founder's claim and one detailed model. Its value is in the arithmetic: it shows how an owner of power, land and manufacturing capacity can enter AI as a compute landlord without training a model, and it sets a price for capacity that other builders will be measured against. The risk is that the revenue depends on a small number of offtakers and on token demand holding at current prices.

Also seen as SpaceX compute revenue, SpaceX ARR

Deeper dive

What it measures. The metric is annual recurring revenue from compute sold to AI customers, and it follows directly from Revenue per gigawatt. Ten gigawatts of capacity multiplied by thirty to fifty million dollars per megawatt-year gives three hundred to five hundred billion dollars, and the modelled figure sits at the lower end to allow for build timing and utilisation. The buyer side is concentrated: the model names Microsoft as the largest offtaker, which makes the revenue a function of a few hyperscaler contracts rather than of a broad market. The company's own claim is narrower, that AI revenue overtakes the space business within the year, which is consistent with a much smaller number than the 2027 model.

Why it matters now. The projection is the strongest version of the argument that the binding constraint on AI is power and construction, not models. A company with launch capacity, manufacturing scale and an appetite for large physical projects can build capacity that cloud providers cannot, including orbital data centres where power and cooling behave differently. If the arithmetic holds, the space business becomes the smaller division of an energy-and-compute company. The financing structure matters too: capacity sold forward to a single large buyer resembles Vendor financing, with the offtaker underwriting the build, and the Gross margin on compute depends on whether the company can generate its own power below grid prices.

Trade-offs. The evidence base is thin: a founder's public claim and a single detailed model, without reported figures. Concentrated offtake means concentrated risk, and a price of thirty to fifty million dollars per megawatt-year assumes frontier-grade demand rather than commodity inference. Ten gigawatts by 2027 requires a construction pace no one has demonstrated. Against that, the company has a record of delivering physical projects that were dismissed as implausible, and the model's authors argue the plan is real on that basis.

What to watch. Any reported AI revenue in the company's disclosures, signed offtake agreements, and whether the per-megawatt price holds as capacity comes online across the industry.

Revenue per gigawatt

Revenue per gigawatt is the annual revenue an AI operator earns per unit of electrical capacity it has built out, with power rather than chips or servers as the denominator.

Vendor financing

Vendor financing is Nvidia using its own balance sheet to sustain demand for its chips, by investing in labs and neoclouds, guaranteeing leases and revenue floors, extending payment terms and financing data centres directly.

Gross margin

Gross margin is revenue minus the direct cost of serving, expressed as a share of revenue; in AI it is the number that says whether selling tokens is a business.
