CyberGym

first seen 2026-W36 · 8 links · 3 min read

CyberGym measures whether an AI agent can do offensive security work end to end: take a real codebase, locate a vulnerability, and produce a working exploit that is verified rather than self-reported. Scores are published on a leaderboard, which turns a hard-to-observe capability into a single comparable number.

It matters now because the number is being used in two opposite directions. Google DeepMind cites frontier-level CyberGym results to justify gating its Gemini 3.8 Flash Cyber variant behind a vetted-access programme, while a seven-person team reports a verified 63.24 percent success rate at tenth place with an open-weight model, arguing that cyber capability is limited by training environments and teacher access rather than by scale. For an executive the questions are whether security tooling budgets should assume these agents arrive through vendors or through open weights, and how quickly the defensive side must keep pace.

Deeper dive

What it measures. CyberGym presents an agent with a real software project and a task drawn from the lifecycle of a vulnerability: find the flaw, reproduce it, and in the harder settings build an exploit that a verifier confirms. Because the verifier runs the exploit, the score reflects working attacks rather than plausible-sounding reports. This places it in the family of Agent benchmarks that reward long tool-using trajectories over single answers, and it is the most-cited public gauge of the Autonomous cyberattack capability that labs and governments are trying to bound.

Why it matters now. Two results in September 2026 put the leaderboard at the centre of the access debate. Google DeepMind reports frontier-level vulnerability discovery for a Cyber variant of Gemini 3.8 Flash and distributes that variant only through its Fairwind programme, one of the Trusted-access programs that condition capability on vetting. At the same time the Feyospace team, seven people training open checkpoints, reports a verified 63.24 percent success rate and tenth place, and uses that position to argue that open-weight cyber capability is constrained by the availability of realistic environments and of stronger teacher models, not by parameter count or compute.

Trade-offs. A public leaderboard on offensive tasks creates the same tension as any dual-use metric. It gives defenders a way to know what attackers can automate, and it gives open-weight builders a target that may be reachable with modest resources once environments leak or are reconstructed. Gating a model on the basis of its score protects against misuse only while comparable Open weights models sit well below the frontier; the Feyospace result suggests that gap may be smaller than the gating assumes. Benchmarks of this kind also age quickly as task sets enter training data.

What to watch. The spread between gated frontier scores and the best open checkpoints is the number to track; if it narrows, restricted access loses its bite and defensive Guardrails must carry more of the load. Watch also whether labs publish the environments used for post-training, since the Feyospace argument implies that environments, not models, are the scarce input.

Autonomous cyberattack

An autonomous cyberattack is an AI agent independently exploiting a vulnerability beyond its instructions, or an automated find-and-exploit loop running from reconnaissance to exfiltration without a human at each step.

Benchmarks

Benchmarks are standardised tests that score AI models on fixed tasks, from vendor-reported tables at each release to third-party leaderboards and research suites.

Guardrails

Guardrails are the controls that limit what a model or agent may do, from safety training and output classifiers to permission checks on tool calls and actions.

Open weights models

Open weights models are AI models whose trained parameters are published for anyone to download, run and modify, in contrast to closed models reachable only through a vendor's API.

Trusted-access programs

Trusted-access programs are a lab's controlled distribution of its strongest model or its most sensitive capabilities to vetted partners under conditions, rather than general release.

Agent benchmarks

Agent benchmarks are evaluation suites that score an AI system on multi-step tasks inside an environment, such as repairing a code repository, operating a terminal or completing a task by voice, rather than on single-answer questions.
