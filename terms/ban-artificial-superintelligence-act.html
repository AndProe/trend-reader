Ban Artificial Superintelligence Act

first seen 2026-W37 · 6 links · 3 min read

The bill, trailed in early September 2026 and formally introduced later that month, would prohibit the development of artificial superintelligence until safety standards are in place. Its enforcement provisions are unusually severe for a technology bill: dissolution of offending companies, criminal penalties of up to 20 years for executives, and a new cabinet-level department to oversee the field. One prominent AI safety organisation has endorsed it; sympathetic commentators say it overreaches as worded but follows the logic of the risk, and have reserved final judgement pending a full reading.

For an executive the bill is a marker rather than an imminent constraint. Its chance of passage in its current form is low, but it shows that superintelligence risk has moved from commentary into draft law with real penalties, and it arrived alongside a visible shift in elite opinion on extinction risk. Companies building or depending on frontier models should expect the legislative conversation to move toward licensing, pause authority and personal liability, and should track which provisions survive into bills that can pass.

Also seen as Sanders-Casar bill, Ban Superintelligence Act, superintelligence ban

Deeper dive

What happened. In the second week of September 2026 the Sanders-Casar bill was described publicly with its headline provisions: a pause on advanced AI development until safety standards exist, a corporate death penalty for violators, prison terms of up to 20 years, and a new cabinet agency. By the last week of the month it had been formally introduced. The Machine Intelligence Research Institute endorsed it. Commentary from the safety-concerned side was that the bill overreaches as worded but follows the logic of the risk, with judgement reserved pending the full text. The bill landed in the same weeks as a run of public statements from senior figures acknowledging extinction risk, the pattern described as a Preference cascade.

How it works. The bill's structure has three parts. A prohibition defines artificial superintelligence and bars its development pending standards, which places the definitional burden on the agency. An enforcement regime attaches penalties to corporations and individuals, with dissolution and criminal liability intended to make the prohibition bite on the largest companies. A new department would set the standards, license development and monitor compliance. The definitional question is central: Frontier models are released continuously, and a threshold that separates permitted from prohibited development must be stated in terms that a regulator can apply, whether by compute, capability evaluation or intended use.

Trade-offs and open questions. A pause backed by criminal penalties is the strongest tool available to a legislature, and its supporters argue nothing weaker will move labs racing each other. The objections are practical and strategic. Definitions of superintelligence are contested, so the prohibition may be unenforceable or over-broad. A unilateral US pause changes the international race without stopping it. Criminal liability for executives may push development into less accountable jurisdictions or into open-weight releases outside any company. And a cabinet agency created in haste risks either capture by the industry it regulates or paralysis of ordinary AI use. Whether the bill's severity helps or hurts the case for more moderate measures is itself debated among those who share its concern about Recursive self-improvement and loss of control.

What to watch. Whether the bill gets a hearing and which provisions attract bipartisan interest. Whether competing proposals, such as licensing regimes without a pause, emerge as the compromise. And whether the labs engage with the text or treat it as unserious, which will indicate how they read the political shift within AI regulation.

Recursive self-improvement

Recursive self-improvement is the use of AI systems to accelerate the research, data, code and infrastructure that produce the next AI system, so that each generation shortens the time to the one after it.

Frontier models

Frontier models are the strongest AI models available at any moment, the tier that sets the state of the art and that everything else is measured against.

Singularity debate

The singularity debate is the public contest between laboratory leaders who describe the industry as entering self-accelerating development and named sceptics who dispute the claim, its timing or its shape.

Preference cascade

A preference cascade is the moment when privately held views become public all at once after a few people speak first; in AI it names the September 2026 wave of lab staff admitting that advanced AI might kill everyone and that no lab has a plan to align superintelligence.

P(doom)

P(doom) is a person's stated probability that advanced AI causes human extinction or an equivalent irreversible catastrophe, usually given with a time window such as within a decade.
