Epoch Capabilities Index

The index combines results from several benchmarks into one capability number intended to be comparable across releases. Its purpose is trend measurement: a single benchmark saturates or gets contaminated, whereas an aggregate degrades more gracefully and can be plotted over years.

Its importance in the vault is its role in assessment design. A proposal for determining whether capability progress has accelerated names aggregate measures of this kind as one of four required evidence sources, alongside intermediate indicators such as pretraining loss, researcher perceptions gathered by interview, and other proxies — on the explicit ground that capability cannot be understood through any single approach. For an executive the useful reading is that nobody credible now treats one index as sufficient, and any acceleration claim resting on a single curve should be discounted.

Also seen as ECI

Deeper dive

How it works. An aggregate index weights several benchmark results into a composite, which smooths the saturation and contamination that affect individual tests. The trade-off is interpretability: a composite movement cannot be attributed to a specific capability without decomposing it, and the weighting embeds a judgment about what matters. That is the same objection the vault records against Artificial Analysis index comparisons, where models sit within a few points of each other while differing by nearly fourfold in cost.

Why it matters now. The question the index is being recruited for is whether AI is accelerating its own development. Detecting that requires separating capability gain from its causes: how much comes from algorithmic progress and data rather than compute scaling, and how much of that is attributable to AI assistance rather than human labour. An index measures the output of that chain, not any link in it, which is why the assessment design pairs it with input measurements and interviews. The link to Recursive self-improvement is therefore methodological: the index is where the effect would show up, not where the cause could be identified.

Trade-offs. Aggregates lag: a composite built from published benchmarks cannot see unreleased internal models, which is precisely the population an acceleration assessment needs. They also inherit every contamination problem of their components. And an honest assessment has to report its own sensitivity — whether its methods would have detected a much larger acceleration had one occurred — which no public index currently does.

What to watch. Whether any index publishes detection-sensitivity analysis, and whether internal capability measures are ever reported alongside public ones.

Benchmarks

Benchmarks are standardised tests that score AI models on fixed tasks, from vendor-reported tables at each release to third-party leaderboards and research suites.

needed by 16 · 39 linkstechnique

Internal benchmarks

Internal benchmarks are evaluations an organisation writes for its own workloads, testing a model or agent on the tasks it will actually perform rather than on public leaderboards.

needed by 1 · 18 linksconcept

Recursive self-improvement

Recursive self-improvement is the use of AI systems to accelerate the research, data, code and infrastructure that produce the next AI system, so that each generation shortens the time to the one after it.

17 linksmetric

Compute efficiency

Compute efficiency is the amount of useful capability delivered per unit of compute, combining gains on the model side, such as fewer active parameters and cheaper inference, with gains on the chip side.

needed by 1 · 10 linksconcept

Frontier models

Frontier models are the strongest AI models available at any moment, the tier that sets the state of the art and that everything else is measured against.

needed by 10 · 29 linksmetric

Humanity's Last Exam

Humanity's Last Exam is a benchmark of expert-level questions across many scientific and humanistic fields, built to remain difficult after earlier suites were saturated.
