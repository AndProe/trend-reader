Complementary assets

first seen 2026-W34 · 4 links · 3 min read

When a technology is easy to copy or licence, the economics of who profits from it depend less on who invented it than on who controls the things it needs to be useful. Those things are the complementary assets. For AI they are compute capacity, proprietary data, distribution channels to customers and, in the enterprise, verified operational knowledge of how a business actually works. A model is rented and getting cheaper; the assets around it are scarce and getting more valuable.

For an executive the concept resolves a common anxiety. The fear that one frontier lab will absorb every industry assumes strong appropriability, meaning the inventor keeps the value. AI has weak appropriability: models leak, open weights follow closed ones within months, and prices fall. The value flows to whoever holds the complements. That argues for investing in data, customer relationships and operational expertise rather than in access to any particular model, and it reframes the moat of a company deploying AI as knowledge of its own domain rather than the model it uses.

Also seen as weak appropriability, complements capture value

Deeper dive

How it works. The framework comes from the economics of innovation. A general-purpose technology creates enormous value, but the inventor captures it only when imitation is hard, through patents, secrecy or lead time. When imitation is easy, the profits go to the owners of the assets needed to commercialise the technology: manufacturing capacity, sales channels, regulatory approvals, installed bases. Applied to AI, the model is the technology and the complements are compute, data and distribution. Compute is scarce and expensive, data that is proprietary and clean is rare, and distribution to enterprises is slow to build.

Why it matters now. The argument was made directly against the claim that one lab would be the only company left: because appropriability is weak, the lab cannot keep the value its models create. The same reasoning supports Compute as currency, the view that once models commoditise, access to compute and energy decides advantage. It is the counterpart to Commoditise your complement: that strategy works by pushing value out of a neighbouring layer, while this framework asks where the value lands, and answers that it lands with the scarce asset regardless of who did the pushing. Practitioners deploying AI into enterprises describe their moat in these terms, as verified operational knowledge that no rented model has, which is also why Token share versus revenue share matters: a lab can own most tokens and still capture a small share of the revenue those tokens enable.

Trade-offs. The framework can be read too comfortably. Compute is a complementary asset, but the labs are among the largest holders of it, so Lab consolidation around compute ownership is consistent with the theory rather than refuted by it. Distribution can be bought by a lab with enough capital. And weak appropriability today does not rule out a capability gap large enough to restore strong appropriability tomorrow, which is the scenario the single-lab argument depends on.

What to watch. Whether frontier labs vertically integrate into compute, data and distribution to capture the complements themselves, how fast open models close the gap with closed ones, and whether enterprise buyers pay for models or for the integrations and knowledge around them.

Compute as currency

Compute as currency is the view that once models become commodities, advantage is decided by access to compute and energy rather than by access to any model, and that compute is increasingly traded, for revenue shares or training tokens, rather than simply sold by the hour.

Lab consolidation

Lab consolidation is the expected shake-out of the frontier model market to a handful of labs and, on the buyer side, one or two vendors per enterprise workload.

Token share versus revenue share

Token share versus revenue share is the distinction between which models process the most tokens and which capture the most money, with open-weight models winning volume while a few closed labs collect most of the revenue.

Commoditise your complement

Commoditise your complement is the strategy of making the product adjacent to your own cheap or free, so that demand for what you actually sell grows.
