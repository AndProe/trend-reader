Inference-time compute

first seen 2026-W32 · needed by 4 · 15 links · 3 min read

A model can be made better in two ways: train it longer, or let it think longer when asked. Inference-time compute is the second. The model generates reasoning tokens before answering, tries several answers and picks the best, verifies its own work, or calls tools, and each of these costs compute at the moment of use. It is OpenAI's strategic bet, it is what makes reasoning effort levels and extended-thinking modes possible, and it is why one mathematical search effort consumed 300 billion output tokens.

For an executive, the shift moves cost from a one-time training bill, paid by the lab, to a per-request bill, paid by the customer. The decisions it touches are the reasoning budget per task, which can be set high for hard problems and low for routine ones; whether to use parallel agents, which act as parallel test-time compute with sublinear speed-up; and how to plan for inference capacity, since mass inference is now the near-term accelerant of AI capability rather than a new training run.

Also seen as test-time compute, inference scaling

Deeper dive

How it works. There are four common forms. Sequential reasoning lets the model emit Reasoning tokens before its answer, working through a problem step by step. Parallel sampling generates several candidates and selects among them by voting or by a verifier. Self-verification spends compute checking an answer, sometimes with a separate reward model that reads the model's hidden states. Tool use spends compute on searches and code execution at answer time. Recurrent depth is the architectural version: the model loops over its own hidden state rather than emitting text. All of these trade extra computation at answer time for accuracy, and the trade is governed by Reasoning effort levels that the user, or increasingly the model itself, sets per request.

Why it matters now. Training compute is expensive to scale and slow to pay off, while inference compute can be scaled per problem. That is why the frontier's hardest results, from claimed proofs of long-standing mathematical problems to multi-day agent runs, are inference-heavy: one search effort reportedly consumed 300 billion output tokens. It also changes economics: Cost per task now depends on how much thinking the task needs, and a cheap model with a large reasoning budget can rival an expensive one at low effort. Multi-agent systems are best understood as parallel test-time compute with sublinear speed-up. Mass inference capacity, rather than recursive self-improvement, is the near-term accelerant of capability, which is why labs are buying inference hardware at the scale they once reserved for training.

Trade-offs and limits. More thinking does not always help: raising the reasoning budget leaves accuracy unchanged on some tasks, and models struggle to know when to stop, so difficulty-aware length control is an active research area. Reasoning tokens add latency, which conflicts with interactive uses. Compaction and caching reduce the cost of long-horizon agents but lose information. And the contrast with Post-training matters: capability bought at inference time must be bought again on every call, whereas capability trained into weights is paid once. Distilling reasoning back into weights closes the loop, with the losses discussed under Lossy self-improvement.

What to watch. Whether models learn to set their own budgets reliably. Whether verification methods make parallel sampling cheap enough for routine use. And whether the inference capacity build-out keeps pace with the reasoning budgets the newest models demand.

Related terms

concept

Reasoning tokens

Reasoning tokens are the internal deliberation a model generates before producing its visible answer, billed and counted like any other output.

6 linksconcept

Reasoning effort levels

Reasoning effort levels are the settings, typically low, medium, high, extra high and max, that control how much internal thinking a model does before answering a request.

9 linkstechnique

Self-verification

Self-verification is an AI system checking its own work before declaring it done, either inside the model's reasoning or through a harness that runs tests, reads telemetry, inspects screenshots and selects among candidate answers.

6 linkstechnique

Test-time training

Test-time training is a model updating its own weights on the input it is currently processing, instead of remaining frozen after training.

needed by 1 · 8 linksmetric

Cost per task

Cost per task is the dollar cost of getting a unit of work actually completed by an AI system, measured at a given quality level, rather than the price per token or the score on a benchmark.

23 linkstechnique

Recurrent depth

Recurrent depth is a looped-transformer architecture in which a model passes activations repeatedly through the same weight-shared block stack, so that additional reasoning happens inside the network rather than as written tokens.
