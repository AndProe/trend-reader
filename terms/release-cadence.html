Release cadence

first seen 2026-W33 · 9 links · 3 min read

Frontier models once arrived a year or more apart. In the agentic era the leading duopoly has been shipping a frontier release roughly every fifty-one days, one lab put out three Flash-class releases in six weeks, and a single week in September 2026 saw four labs ship updates at once. Chinese labs compete primarily on this axis, turning around releases in days where Western labs take months.

For an executive the cadence changes procurement and risk. A model evaluated in a bake-off may be superseded before the contract is signed, so vendor selection has to weigh the lab's trajectory rather than a single snapshot. Point releases marketed as production-ready one generation after launch shift stability work onto the customer. The pre-release testing window compresses as the cadence rises, which is the regulatory concern behind pacing proposals, and constant updates produce fatigue in the teams expected to re-validate their systems each time.

Also seen as time to release, pre-release testing window, shipping cadence

Deeper dive

How it works. Cadence is set by how quickly a lab can complete a post-training cycle, run its evaluations and clear release review. Large pretraining runs remain infrequent, but most shipped improvement now comes from post-training and reinforcement learning, which take weeks rather than months, and from distilling a flagship into smaller Flash-class variants. The result is a stream of point releases between generations. Labs also report the compounding effect: a third-generation specialised model described as five times sharper than the second, one year on, is the product of many intermediate updates rather than one leap.

Why it matters now. The quantified figure for the leading duopoly is one frontier release every fifty-one days. Chinese labs have made cadence their main competitive tool, and the Open-closed capability gap is largely a story of how fast open releases follow closed ones, with Distillation attacks on proprietary reasoning traces as one accelerant. The same speed produces model fatigue among developers, since each release invalidates prior evaluations and prompts, and it raises the question of how AI regulation can require meaningful pre-deployment testing when the testing window is shorter than the review process.

Trade-offs. Fast cadence keeps a lab visible and lets it respond to competitor releases within weeks, but it shortens internal safety testing, encourages Benchmaxxing as each release seeks a headline score, and pushes production hardening onto customers when a point release is labelled production-ready. It also strains the supply of training environments and evaluation data, since each cycle consumes what the RL data industry can produce. Slower cadence buys testing time and stability at the cost of perceived momentum.

What to watch. Whether the fifty-one-day interval holds or compresses further, whether any lab formally commits to a minimum testing window, and whether enterprise buyers begin to price release stability into contracts through long-term-support versions.
