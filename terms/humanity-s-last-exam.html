The benchmark was created in response to models saturating earlier academic suites: its questions span advanced chemistry, computer science, mathematics, classical literature and ecology, and were written to be hard for specialists. Its value as an instrument comes from the trajectory rather than from any single score — top systems moved from 8 percent at its January 2025 launch to 67.7 percent by October 2026.

That curve is now load-bearing in public arguments about acceleration, which is a reason to be careful with it. A multiple-choice or short-answer exam measures recall and reasoning on questions with known answers; it does not measure whether a system can extend the frontier of knowledge, and the gap between those two is exactly what the acceleration argument asserts. The benchmark's own name concedes the problem it will eventually have: like its predecessors, its usefulness ends when it saturates, and at two-thirds correct that point is visible.

Deeper dive

How it works. Items are expert-written questions with verifiable answers, drawn across disciplines to prevent a single domain's progress from dominating the aggregate. Scoring is item-level accuracy, which makes the metric easy to compare across systems and time but insensitive to how an answer was reached — the same limitation that Agent benchmarks work has been pushing against for trajectory-based tasks.

Why it matters now. The score series is the quantitative spine of the case that models are reaching the frontier of human science, cited alongside mathematics-specific suites moving from 2 to 94 percent. It is also the metric most exposed to the measurement problem the same period has produced elsewhere: one long-horizon benchmark was retired because its method could no longer measure the quantity it targeted, and an unpublished theoretical-physics evaluation saw a model's score rise after experts revised their own answers under the model's objection. In both cases capability outran the instrument, and the second case makes adjudication rather than accuracy the deciding variable.

Trade-offs. Expert-written answer keys can be wrong, and at high model scores the residual errors increasingly include key errors rather than model errors — which inflates apparent difficulty while deflating measured capability, then reverses on correction. Contamination is a standing risk for any published suite. And a high score on questions with known answers remains weak evidence about work with no answer key, which is the capability the benchmark is usually invoked to support.

What to watch. Whether the remaining third resolves to genuine difficulty or to key errors, what replaces the suite once it saturates, and whether evaluations with no published answers — unpublished problem sets, held-out suites the system is unaware of — displace it as the credible instrument.

Benchmarks

Benchmarks are standardised tests that score AI models on fixed tasks, from vendor-reported tables at each release to third-party leaderboards and research suites.

Frontier models

Frontier models are the strongest AI models available at any moment, the tier that sets the state of the art and that everything else is measured against.

Epoch Capabilities Index

The Epoch Capabilities Index is an aggregate measure of model capability built from multiple benchmarks, used to track progress over time rather than to rank a single release.

AI mathematical discovery

Models producing new mathematical results rather than reproducing known ones, most visibly the prime-gap bounds attributed to GPT-6 Astra and the Navier-Stokes proof that followed a week later.

needed by 1 · 12 linksconcept

Benchmaxxing

Benchmaxxing is the practice of tuning a model release so it scores well on the industry-standard public test sets, which is legitimate and expected rather than fraudulent.

14 linksproduct

GDPval

GDPval is OpenAI's benchmark for real-world, computer-based knowledge work, reported as an Elo score that ranks models by how often their output is preferred on economically valuable tasks.
