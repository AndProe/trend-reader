Capability overhang

first seen 2026-W38 · 9 links · 3 min read

Capability overhang names a simple observation: the models organisations already pay for can do far more than those organisations use them for. Most workflows, staff skills and procurement rules were designed for models two or three generations old, so each new release widens the gap rather than closing it. A slowdown in model progress would not erase the overhang; it would take years of adoption work to catch up with what is already available.

The term also carries a strategic reading. Once models have improved past the point where customers will trade other terms for a better one, the frontier stops commanding a premium. Buyers optimise on price, reliability, data terms and integration instead. For an executive the two readings point to the same conclusion: the return on adoption effort, training and process redesign is now higher than the return on waiting for a better model, and the value of a frontier-model contract should be judged against what the organisation can absorb, not what the model can do.

Also seen as overhang, capability overhang (adoption)

Deeper dive

How it works. Model capability advances in discrete releases, while organisational absorption advances through hiring, training, process change and tool integration, all of which move slowly. The overhang is the integral of that mismatch. It is visible in Enterprise AI adoption data as a large share of licensed seats used for chat-style tasks that a much older model handled equally well, and in AI productivity studies where measured gains lag the capability improvements that should have produced them.

Why it matters now. Two readings of the overhang have surfaced in the same month. The adoption reading treats it as the central problem for the next several years: the bottleneck is not model quality but the human and institutional work needed to use it, and it argues against expecting capability slowdowns to matter much. The strategy reading argues that Frontier models have improved beyond what customers will pay extra for, so holding the frontier is worth less than it was. That interacts with Frontier model pricing, where cache discounts and price wars now do more work than capability claims, and with the logic of Commoditise your complement, in which whoever captures the workflow can treat the model as an interchangeable input.

Trade-offs. Investing heavily in adoption while the overhang is large gives durable returns but risks building process around capabilities that the next release makes obsolete. Waiting for models to become easier to use is cheaper in the short term but compounds the gap. There is also an organisational cost: a persistent overhang produces AI fatigue among staff who are told each month that the tools can do more but see no change in their own work.

What to watch. Whether enterprise usage measured in tasks completed, rather than seats licensed, begins to climb toward what models can do, whether labs respond to the strategy reading by competing on terms other than capability, and whether the overhang narrows in specific functions such as software engineering while staying wide elsewhere.
