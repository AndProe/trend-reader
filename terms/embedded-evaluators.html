Embedded evaluators

first seen 2026-W38 · 11 links · 3 min read

An embedded evaluator is an outside expert who works inside a lab, sees what employees see, including unreleased models, training runs and incident reports, and can publish conclusions without the lab's veto. The proposal appeared in a frontier-lab chief executive's essay on pacing the frontier, and both Anthropic and OpenAI have since committed to it; Anthropic's own account of measuring the pace of development inside labs names it as a stated plan.

It matters now because it is the oversight mechanism most likely to exist in practice. Regulation is stalled and politically contested, and one senior US official opposes even voluntary evaluators as a rerun of content moderation. The practical problems are staffing, since qualified evaluators are scarce; trust, since labs must let outsiders into their most sensitive work; and enforcement, since an evaluator who publishes something damaging may find access withdrawn or their findings softened. For an executive, the presence of embedded evaluators will become a due-diligence signal about a vendor, and their reports a new class of disclosure.

Also seen as embedded third-party evaluators, embedded auditors

Deeper dive

How it works. The arrangement gives a third-party organisation a standing presence inside the lab rather than a periodic audit: evaluators hold internal accounts, observe training runs and pre-release model behaviour, receive incident reports, and retain the right to publish findings. It extends the pre-deployment testing that external evaluators already perform on a per-model basis into continuous access, and it is designed to work with Alignment evaluation as the discipline and AI incident transparency as the output. It forms one pillar of Pacing the frontier, the proposal that labs coordinate to slow releases while independent parties verify what they are doing.

Why it matters now. Two labs have committed, one of them naming it in a public plan for measuring the pace of internal development. The debate has moved from principle to mechanics. Evaluator supply is read as the binding constraint: few people combine the technical depth to assess a frontier model with the independence to report on it. The trust problem cuts both ways, since the lab exposes its most sensitive work and the evaluator's credibility depends on not being captured. And enforcement is unresolved: an exchange in which an auditor's findings were softened is cited as the case for guaranteed publication rights, while opponents inside government describe even voluntary evaluators as a new trust-and-safety bureaucracy and one placement has been attacked as ideological.

Trade-offs. Continuous access improves what evaluators can see at the cost of making them dependent on the lab for that access, which is the mechanism of Regulatory capture in miniature. Publication rights protect independence but give labs a reason to limit what evaluators are shown. The model also assumes that the labs' willingness holds; it is a voluntary commitment, and it sits alongside proposals for an Antitrust exemption that would let labs coordinate on safety, which critics read as coordination on competition.

What to watch. Who the first embedded evaluators are and what they publish, whether a lab ever withdraws access after an unfavourable report, and whether Lab oversight capacity grows fast enough to staff more than two labs.

Related terms

concept

Alignment evaluation

Alignment evaluation is the testing of whether a model behaves as its developers intend, covering deception, sabotage, reward hacking and other misbehaviour, as distinct from testing what it can do.

AI incident transparency

The demand that labs disclose what happened in AI incidents in enough detail to be verified: exact prompts, model lineage, timelines and the artefacts involved, rather than summaries.

13 linksconcept

Human oversight

Human oversight is the practice of keeping a person in the loop over an agent's actions, with authority to approve, correct or stop them, and the review burden that this imposes.

15 linksconcept

Lab oversight capacity

Lab oversight capacity is the ability of a frontier laboratory to monitor what its own models and agents are doing in deployment and in internal use.

8 linksconcept

Trusted-access programs

Trusted-access programs are a lab's controlled distribution of its strongest model or its most sensitive capabilities to vetted partners under conditions, rather than general release.

needed by 1 · 10 linksconcept

Antitrust exemption

An antitrust exemption is a carve-out from competition law that would let rival AI labs coordinate on safety, such as cross-testing each other's models, agreeing release timing or a coordinated slowdown, without exposure to liability for restraint of trade.
