ClusterMAX grades GPU cloud providers on how well their clusters actually run. The premise is that renting accelerators is not a commodity transaction: identical chips behave very differently depending on networking, storage, scheduling, failure handling and support, and a buyer comparing hourly prices is comparing the least informative number available.

For anyone buying training or inference capacity this is the practical counterpart to the efficiency metrics the vault tracks on the hardware side. Tokens per watt describes what a chip can do; a provider rating describes whether a renter will get it. The gap between the two is where a large share of real compute spend is lost, and it is why a cheaper quoted rate from a weaker operator frequently costs more in practice.

Also seen as ClusterMAX rating

Deeper dive

How it works. A rating of this kind assesses the operational layer around the accelerators: interconnect quality and topology, storage throughput, how quickly failed nodes are detected and replaced, scheduler behaviour under contention, and the responsiveness of support. These determine utilisation, and utilisation determines the effective price of a GPU-hour far more than the quoted rate does. That makes it the demand-side complement to Performance per watt, which measures the hardware rather than the service.

Why it matters now. Capacity is scarce in a way that rewards weak operators: the vault records specific CPU and GPU shortages, regions closed to new tenants, reservations requiring months of lead time, and customers prepaying for capacity not yet online. In that market a buyer's leverage comes from knowing which suppliers deliver, which is exactly what a public rating supplies. It also bears on Neo clouds as a category — a tier of providers whose economics depend on renting the same chips as everyone else, so operational quality is their only durable differentiator.

Trade-offs. A single published rating is a judgment by one party, and the methodology behind operational grades is harder to audit than a benchmark score. Ratings also age quickly: a provider's quality changes with each buildout, and scarcity gives even poorly rated operators customers. And because the vault's references to it are secondary, it has no independent assessment of how the grades are derived.

What to watch. Whether buyers cite ratings in procurement, whether providers respond to low grades, and whether any independent methodology emerges.

Related terms

concept

Neo clouds

Neo clouds are GPU-rental cloud providers outside the hyperscalers, such as CoreWeave and the inference hosts Together, Fireworks, Modal and Baseten, that lease accelerators and serve open models.

needed by 1 · 10 linksconcept

GPU access

GPU access is the question of who can obtain frontier compute and on what terms, from hyperscalers that lock up the supply pipeline to everyone outside it facing scarcity.

needed by 1 · 9 linksconcept

Hyperscalers

Hyperscalers are the largest cloud providers, AWS, Azure and Google Cloud, together with Oracle and increasingly the frontier labs themselves, that buy most AI accelerators, issue most AI-related debt and build the gigawatt campuses.

needed by 1 · 18 linksconcept

AI infrastructure debt

The borrowing required to build AI data centres, estimated at roughly $4 trillion over five years, and the instruments that carry it: project finance at 65 to 75 percent leverage, off-balance-sheet vehicles, private credit, commercial paper, corporate and municipal bonds.

needed by 1 · 10 linksmetric

Gross margin

Gross margin is revenue minus the direct cost of serving, expressed as a share of revenue; in AI it is the number that says whether selling tokens is a business.

Performance per watt

Performance per watt measures how much useful AI output, typically tokens or benchmark-weighted intelligence, a system produces per unit of electrical power.
