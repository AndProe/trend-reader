Classifier fallback

first seen 2026-W36 · 6 links · 3 min read

Anthropic's most capable models ship behind a set of safety classifiers covering areas such as cybersecurity, biology and distillation. When one of those classifiers fires on a request to Fable 5.1, the request is not simply refused; it is passed to an older model, in practice Opus 4.8, which answers it instead. The user gets a response, but from a weaker model, often without realising the switch has happened.

This is the main practical complaint about Fable 5.1 among heavy users, and it is also a security concern. In the system card's browser prompt-injection tests, 21 of 29 successful attacks landed on the fallback model rather than the primary one, 20 of them on Opus 4.8. An executive evaluating the model for agentic use should know that the guardrails on the headline model do not fully extend to what actually answers when a classifier fires, and should ask for the option to disable fallback or route to a stronger fallback such as Opus 5.

Also seen as fallback model, fallback to Opus 4.8

Deeper dive

How it works. A frontier deployment runs classifiers alongside the model. Each classifier covers a domain, and there are five areas with different fallback behaviours. When a classifier triggers, the system routes the request to a designated fallback model, one that either lacks the dangerous capability or is judged safe to answer in that domain. The design goal is to preserve availability: a user asking a borderline question gets an answer rather than a refusal. This is distinct from Smart model routing, which chooses a model on cost or capability grounds before the request is processed; fallback is triggered by a safety judgement after the fact.

Why it matters now. Claude Fable 5.1 shipped with this mechanism, and the same pattern appears with the Enterprise Frontier Safeguards attached to later releases, where the named safeguard domains are cybersecurity, biology and distillation. The security cost is documented in the system card: most successful browser prompt-injection attacks hit the fallback, and the fallback is an older model with weaker resistance. One reading treats this as an intended safety property, the more capable decider handing back what it will not decide; another treats it as a hole, because an attacker who can trigger a classifier can steer a session to the weaker model. Both readings are compatible; the mechanism trades capability risk for injection risk.

Trade-offs. Refusing outright is safer against injection but produces the Guardrails tax of lost throughput and irritated users. Falling back to a strong recent model such as Claude Opus 5 reduces the injection gap but may reintroduce the capability the classifier was meant to gate. Disabling fallback for agentic sessions, which some users want as an option, puts the decision back with the customer. Any choice interacts with Refusal behaviour, since a model that refuses less needs classifiers more.

What to watch. Whether Anthropic exposes fallback configuration to enterprise customers, the share of successful attacks landing on fallback models in the next system card for Mythos 5.1 and its successors, and whether other labs adopt a similar mechanism or keep refusals as the default.
