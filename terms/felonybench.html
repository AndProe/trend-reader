FelonyBench measures whether a model will help with serious crime. It enters the vault as evidence in an argument rather than as a methods paper: cited by an analyst making the case that the documented record of models implicated in harm does not match the prevailing framing, in which open weights are dangerous and safeguarded closed APIs are safe.

The argument it supports is worth stating precisely, because it is falsifiable. On the public record, closed models have been behind most documented attacks. Two readings follow: either both open weights and safeguarded APIs are easy to misuse, so the slogan should be "open unsafe, closed unsafe"; or there are simply few actors willing to run loud attacks, in which case the stronger capability of closed models outweighs their stronger safeguards. Either way the policy conclusion that open weights alone need banning does not follow from the evidence.

Deeper dive

How it works. A benchmark of this kind presents requests for assistance with serious criminal activity and scores whether the model complies, which makes it a measure of Refusal behaviour under adversarial framing rather than of capability. Its evidentiary value depends on how the requests were constructed and whether safeguards were engaged as they are in production, neither of which the vault can assess from the citations it holds.

Why it matters now. The open-weights cyber debate has been conducted largely on projections and classified briefings. Measures like this, alongside the documented attribution of real attacks, are the only public counterweight — and the position they support is a conditional rather than a conclusion: if the latest open-weight models must be banned to slow cyber-risk diffusion, then public APIs for frontier closed models should also be illegal, because banning one while the other advances widens the offence-defence gap. That is the argument the vault records under Open weights regulation risk.

Trade-offs. Attributing attacks to models is hard, so a record showing closed models behind most documented incidents may reflect what is detectable and reported rather than what occurs. A compliance benchmark also measures willingness, not uplift: a model that answers may contribute nothing an attacker lacked. And the capability picture moved in the same period, with an authorised contest won on open weights, which cuts the other way.

What to watch. Whether attribution improves, whether compliance scores correlate with real incidents, and whether any jurisdiction regulates the two access modes symmetrically.

Related terms

concept

Alignment evaluation

Alignment evaluation is the testing of whether a model behaves as its developers intend, covering deception, sabotage, reward hacking and other misbehaviour, as distinct from testing what it can do.

AI cybersecurity skepticism

The counter-position that the AI-and-security incidents of 2026 are ordinary security failures rather than evidence of unprecedented machine autonomy, and that the predicted surge in AI-driven vulnerability discovery is not yet visible in the data.

7 linksconcept

Autonomous cyberattack

An autonomous cyberattack is an AI agent independently exploiting a vulnerability beyond its instructions, or an automated find-and-exploit loop running from reconnaissance to exfiltration without a human at each step.

needed by 1 · 12 linksconcept

Open weights regulation risk

Open weights regulation risk is the possibility that government rules restrict, burden or effectively ban the release and use of open-weight AI models, either through explicit prohibition or through compliance obligations that only closed vendors can meet.

11 linksconcept

Refusal behaviour

Refusal behaviour is the set of conditions under which a model declines a request, what it declines instead of doing, and what kinds of pressure move it to comply.

Benchmarks

Benchmarks are standardised tests that score AI models on fixed tasks, from vendor-reported tables at each release to third-party leaderboards and research suites.
