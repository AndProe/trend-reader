Grid interconnection

first seen 2026-W33 · 7 links · 3 min read

A grid interconnection is the permission and physical works that let a new large load, such as a data centre, or a new generator draw from or feed into the utility grid. Grid operators process requests through a queue with studies of grid impact, and the queue now stretches for years. Fast-track schemes announced to speed it up have, to date, not energised a single megawatt, and Texas has paused the next step of its ERCOT queue as a state-level instrument of control.

For an executive planning AI capacity, interconnection timing, not chip supply or capital, is often the schedule-critical path. The alternatives are behind-the-meter generation, which trades grid dependence for the cost and permitting of on-site power, or siting where queues are shorter. The risk side is political: rising electricity prices and local backlash are making operators and legislators less willing to prioritise data centre connections.

Also seen as interconnection queue, interconnection fast-tracks, grid interconnections

Deeper dive

How it works. A grid operator such as PJM or ERCOT receives interconnection requests, studies each one for its effect on the transmission system, assigns any upgrade costs, and only then approves a connection. Requests are processed roughly in order, and each study depends on the projects ahead of it, so withdrawals and re-studies ripple through the queue. For a data centre the process determines when it can draw power at all; for a generator it determines when the new supply reaches the grid. In practice, cheap interconnections have disappeared because the easy transmission headroom has been consumed, and building new grid-connected generation takes five years or more.

Why it binds now. Hyperscalers and labs are planning gigawatt-scale campuses whose schedules are set by power, not by chips. Interconnection fast-tracks have been announced but have not energised a megawatt, and Texas has paused the next step of the ERCOT queue, which shows that states will use the queue as a policy lever. Meanwhile the modelling and cost-allocation decisions grid operators make about capacity feed directly into Electricity prices, and a modelling mistake in PJM's approach has been blamed for billions in ratepayer costs. That link between data centre demand and household bills is fuelling Data center backlash.

Trade-offs. Behind-the-meter power is the route around the queue: on-site gas turbines, batteries or other generation that serve the data centre without a grid draw. It removes the waiting but adds fuel logistics, air permits, equipment lead times and, often, a later grid connection anyway. Siting in regions with shorter queues moves the problem rather than solving it, and it can put capacity far from customers and staff. Every year of delay also affects the economics measured by Revenue per gigawatt, since capital committed to unpowered sites earns nothing.

What to watch. Whether any fast-track scheme energises real capacity. How ERCOT's pause resolves and whether other states follow. And whether operators reform queue processing to study clusters rather than projects, which is the reform most likely to shorten the wait.

Electricity prices

Electricity prices, as an AI-infrastructure metric, are the retail and wholesale power costs that data-centre demand is accused of pushing up, and that in turn drive public opposition to new sites.

Data center backlash

Data center backlash is the local, state and electoral opposition to large AI data centres, driven by concern over electricity bills, water use, emissions and grid strain.

8 linksconcept

Hyperscalers

Hyperscalers are the largest cloud providers, AWS, Azure and Google Cloud, together with Oracle and increasingly the frontier labs themselves, that buy most AI accelerators, issue most AI-related debt and build the gigawatt campuses.

Revenue per gigawatt

Revenue per gigawatt is the annual revenue an AI operator earns per unit of electrical capacity it has built out, with power rather than chips or servers as the denominator.

8 linksconcept

AI infrastructure debt

The borrowing required to build AI data centres, estimated at roughly $4 trillion over five years, and the instruments that carry it: project finance at 65 to 75 percent leverage, off-balance-sheet vehicles, private credit, commercial paper, corporate and municipal bonds.

Behind-the-meter power

Behind-the-meter power is electricity generation built on the data-centre site itself, bypassing the grid, its interconnection queue and its capacity markets.
