AgentX belongs to the family of agentic benchmarks that score a system on completing work rather than on answering a question. Its appearances in the vault are through analyst coverage rather than through a methods paper, so what is recorded here is its role in comparisons, not an independent assessment of its design.

Its relevance is that agent rankings are now quoted as readily as model rankings, while the evidence base behind them is weaker. Controlled work in the same period found that agent scores move substantially with the harness, that model rankings reverse between harnesses, and that typical benchmark sizes cannot resolve the differences being advertised. Any single agentic benchmark should therefore be read as one configuration's result, not as a property of the model.

Also seen as AgentX

Deeper dive

How it works. An agentic benchmark gives a system a task, a set of tools and an environment, then scores the outcome — in better designs with partial credit for intermediate progress rather than a terminal pass or fail. That design choice matters: the vault records a case where binary scoring averaged 0.221 against 0.653 under dense scoring on the same tasks, which means the grading regime can change a conclusion by a factor of three.

Why it matters now. Agentic capability is the axis vendors are competing on, so the measures used to compare it carry unusual weight. The vault's material shows three independent problems with those measures: harness choice moves scores as much as rerunning the same configuration does, so comparisons across harnesses are not interpretable; scores reverse between harnesses for the same model pair; and common task-set sizes lack the statistical power to detect the gaps being claimed. Those apply to Agent benchmarks generally rather than to this suite specifically.

Trade-offs. Because its appearances here are secondary, the vault cannot say how this suite handles the design questions that distinguish the better agentic benchmarks — partial credit, contamination control, calibration against a scripted baseline, and whether simulated time is decoupled from model latency. Where those are absent, a benchmark tends to reward fast models and penalise thorough ones. Comparisons against a scripted policy are the most informative control the vault has seen, since they separate agentic competence from mere capability.

What to watch. Whether the suite publishes rerun calibration and power analysis, and whether its rankings survive a harness swap.

Related terms

technique

Agent benchmarks

Agent benchmarks are evaluation suites that score an AI system on multi-step tasks inside an environment, such as repairing a code repository, operating a terminal or completing a task by voice, rather than on single-answer questions.

needed by 7 · 23 linksproduct

Terminal-Bench

Terminal-Bench is a benchmark of command-line tasks that scores coding agents end to end, with version 2.1 comprising 87 tasks.

15 linksconcept

Agent reliability

Agent reliability is whether an agent completes a given workflow acceptably often enough to be trusted with it, measured as a target such as success on nearly every run under a defined oversight policy and cost, rather than as a best-case capability score.

11 linksconcept

Long-horizon goals

Long-horizon goals are objectives that an agent pursues autonomously over days, maintaining coherence across billions of tokens and many sub-tasks rather than completing one request and stopping.

needed by 1 · 13 linkstechnique

Benchmarks

Benchmarks are standardised tests that score AI models on fixed tasks, from vendor-reported tables at each release to third-party leaderboards and research suites.

needed by 14 · 35 linkstechnique

Internal benchmarks

Internal benchmarks are evaluations an organisation writes for its own workloads, testing a model or agent on the tasks it will actually perform rather than on public leaderboards.
