Energy efficiency

first seen 2026-W36 · 4 links · 3 min read

Energy efficiency measures how much useful model output a system produces for each joule of electricity it draws. In research it is reported as tokens per joule or as GPU energy per benchmark task, and one sustainability framework for edge models treats measured energy as a first-class pillar alongside accuracy and cost. It is the research-side twin of performance per watt, which is the hardware vendor's framing of the same quantity.

It matters now because energy is where AI's costs and constraints meet. In the data centre, power is the input that is hardest to add and most politically exposed, so a model that does the same work for less energy is directly cheaper to serve. On small devices the constraint is physical: in AI headphones, every cubic millimetre reclaimed from other components buys battery capacity, and efficiency buys runtime. For an executive the metric belongs in vendor comparisons next to price per token, because energy is what the grid and the regulator will eventually charge for.

Also seen as energy per token, GPU energy

Deeper dive

What it measures. Energy efficiency is output per joule. At the system level it is measured by metering GPU or accelerator power during a workload and dividing by the work done, giving tokens per joule for inference or energy per training step. A triple-bottom-line framework for edge language models puts measured GPU energy alongside accuracy and cost as one of three pillars, so that a model is judged on what it costs the environment as well as what it scores. Hardware vendors report the same idea as Performance per watt; the difference is that the research metric is measured end to end on a real workload rather than quoted from a spec sheet.

Why it matters now. Energy is the input that AI infrastructure cannot easily buy more of. Power is the constraint on new sites and the source of public opposition, so improvements in energy per token are worth as much as improvements in raw throughput. The architectural work is responding: heterogeneous systems that split attention and feed-forward work across different hardware, and subquadratic attention variants, are being evaluated on tokens per joule rather than only on latency. On devices the argument is more physical still: for headphones the binding constraint is mechanical volume, and the currency that efficiency buys is battery capacity.

Trade-offs. Efficiency gains from Quantization and Small language models come with accuracy costs, and the correct comparison is energy per correct answer, not energy per token. A model that emits fewer tokens to reach the same result is more efficient in the sense that matters, which connects the metric to Compute efficiency and to token-frugal reasoning. Measurement is also unstandardised: reported figures depend on batch size, hardware generation and whether idle power is counted, so cross-paper comparisons are unreliable.

What to watch. Whether energy per task becomes a reported figure on model cards and in procurement, whether grid operators or regulators begin to price AI load on efficiency, and whether edge hardware vendors publish tokens per joule as a headline number in the way they now publish TOPS.

Quantization

Quantization reduces the numeric precision of a model's weights and activations after training, for example from 16-bit to 8-bit or 4-bit formats, so that it uses less memory, bandwidth and energy.

7 linksconcept

Small language models

Small language models are models designed to be small from the start, typically from around one to fourteen billion parameters, rather than large models compressed after training.

8 linksmetric

Compute efficiency

Compute efficiency is the amount of useful capability delivered per unit of compute, combining gains on the model side, such as fewer active parameters and cheaper inference, with gains on the chip side.
