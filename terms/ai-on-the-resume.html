AI on the resume

first seen 2026-W33 · 5 links · 3 min read

AI on the resume is the career-side consequence of the shift to agent-driven engineering. Two years ago experience with AI coding tools was a plus; in 2026 experienced engineering leaders describe it as a requirement, and advise anyone who has been sceptical to get practical experience quickly, because hiring managers now screen for it and its absence reads as a signal of disengagement.

The effect on careers is concrete. Engineering leaders taking career breaks find that the market they return to expects demonstrable, current AI practice, not familiarity. Managers are moving back to individual-contributor roles partly to rebuild that practice, and executives are picking up the tools themselves for the same reason. For a company this cuts both ways: it is a cheap screening criterion, but it also creates pressure for people to perform AI usage rather than exercise judgement about it, and it can tip into the exhaustion that mandated adoption produces. The screen should test for outcomes, not for enthusiasm.

Also seen as AI experience requirement

Deeper dive

What has changed. The requirement has shifted from awareness to practice. A candidate is expected to describe how they use agents in their own work, what they trust them with, where they have seen them fail and how they verify output. For leaders the bar is higher: they are expected to have shaped how their teams adopt the tools, with evidence, which is hard to have if they have spent the period managing rather than building. That is a driver of the Manager-to-IC moves that senior engineers are making, and of the return of Hands-on executives who use the tools themselves rather than commissioning reports about them.

Why it matters now. The labour market for engineers has bifurcated. Demand is strong for people with frontier tooling experience and weak for people without it, and the gap is visible in the difficulty engineering leaders report when returning from career breaks. The advice from practitioners who were themselves sceptics is blunt: the scepticism was reasonable, the tools have moved, and continuing to abstain is now a career risk. This sits inside Engineering culture as the norm that every engineer is expected to work with agents.

Trade-offs. A screen for AI experience is easy to apply and easy to game. It rewards visible usage, which shades into Tokenmaxxing, and it can penalise people whose judgement about when not to use a tool is exactly what a team needs. The contrast with AI fatigue is the point: the same expectation that makes AI experience mandatory produces burnout when it becomes a quota. Companies that hire for AI enthusiasm rather than for demonstrated results with AI tend to get the enthusiasm.

What to watch. Whether interviews move to practical assessments of agent-assisted work, whether the requirement spreads beyond engineering to product, design and management roles, and whether the leaders who stepped away in 2025 find a way back.

Manager-to-IC moves

Manager-to-IC moves are engineering leaders voluntarily stepping back from management into individual-contributor roles, typically for stability or to build hands-on AI skills.

Hands-on executives

Hands-on executives are founders, directors and vice presidents who build with AI tools themselves rather than delegating all technical work, often overriding the leaders beneath them.

Tokenmaxxing

Tokenmaxxing is engineers inflating their AI usage, measured in tokens or tool invocations, to score well on adoption metrics rather than to do better work.

Engineering culture

Engineering culture is how an engineering organisation actually works: the goodwill, autonomy, experimentation and internal sharing of practice that decide what gets built and how well.
