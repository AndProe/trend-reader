Compute futures

Capacity contracts are rectangles: a fixed number of accelerators for a fixed period. Demand is not — it grows, spikes and falls through the day. Anyone who buys the rectangle either pays for idle hardware or misses demand, and that mismatch is the problem a financial market would address.

The pressure has become acute in a specific way. With capacity scarce, providers extended credit risk rather than price, so buyers who are not investment grade are asked for ever larger prepayments — reportedly as high as the full contract value upfront, because neither the lender nor the cloud could absorb more risk. The consequence is that startups may be unable to buy compute at all, not because they cannot pay the price but because they cannot post the collateral. Practitioners have framed this as a species of interest-rate rise and proposed forward and futures markets as the fix: a tradeable instrument lets the risk sit with whoever is willing to hold it rather than with the buyer who needs the capacity.

Also seen as GPU futures, compute forward market, tradeable compute

Deeper dive

How it works. Two complementary approaches have emerged. One makes capacity tradeable — a market where contracts change hands and settle physically, so a holder who no longer needs capacity can sell it rather than waste it. The other makes capacity usable in smaller elastic slices: pool bursty demand from many customers against longer-term secured supply underneath, which is duration transformation in the banking sense. The first addresses who bears the price risk; the second addresses who bears the utilisation risk. A functioning futures market would let both be priced separately, which is the point of commoditising.

Why it matters now. Two measurements make the inefficiency concrete. A survey of companies running AI found a large majority leaving accelerators idle at least half the time while most still planned further buildout — overbought peaks. And the prepayment demands described above show the financing cost of the same mismatch falling on the least creditworthy buyers. Those are the two halves of a market without instruments: waste on one side, exclusion on the other.

Trade-offs. Whoever pools demand carries the price risk: lock in capacity at today's prices and a subsequent fall leaves you holding expensive supply, which operators acknowledge as a real exposure even while not forecasting a collapse. Financialisation also invites the usual hazards — speculative positions in an asset whose physical delivery depends on power, water and construction schedules that no contract can accelerate. And a futures market prices a commodity, while accelerators are not fungible across generations, interconnects or software stacks, so standardisation is the hard part rather than the trading.

What to watch. Whether prepayment demands ease as instruments appear, whether any contract achieves enough standardisation to trade at volume, and whether the idle-capacity share falls — the measurable test of whether a market is doing its job.

concept

Compute as currency

Compute as currency is the view that once models become commodities, advantage is decided by access to compute and energy rather than by access to any model, and that compute is increasingly traded, for revenue shares or training tokens, rather than simply sold by the hour.

GPU access

GPU access is the question of who can obtain frontier compute and on what terms, from hyperscalers that lock up the supply pipeline to everyone outside it facing scarcity.

needed by 2 · 13 linksconcept

Neo clouds

Neo clouds are GPU-rental cloud providers outside the hyperscalers, such as CoreWeave and the inference hosts Together, Fireworks, Modal and Baseten, that lease accelerators and serve open models.

needed by 1 · 11 linksmetric

Revenue per gigawatt

Revenue per gigawatt is the annual revenue an AI operator earns per unit of electrical capacity it has built out, with power rather than chips or servers as the denominator.

9 linksconcept

AI infrastructure debt

The borrowing required to build AI data centres, estimated at roughly $4 trillion over five years, and the instruments that carry it: project finance at 65 to 75 percent leverage, off-balance-sheet vehicles, private credit, commercial paper, corporate and municipal bonds.

needed by 1 · 11 linksmetric

Gross margin

Gross margin is revenue minus the direct cost of serving, expressed as a share of revenue; in AI it is the number that says whether selling tokens is a business.
