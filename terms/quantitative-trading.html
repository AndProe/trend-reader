Quantitative trading

first seen 2026-W33 · 5 links · 3 min read

Quantitative trading firms, also called proprietary trading firms or prop shops, trade with their own money rather than clients'. Their systems watch market data, decide whether to act, and send orders, and for years the winning edge was doing that loop faster than anyone else, measured in microseconds. The current era is different: raw speed is largely commoditised, and the edge comes from better models of what will happen next.

For an executive outside finance the sector matters for two reasons. It competes directly with AI labs for the same engineers, paying top of market and offering a culture of deep technical rigour, which shapes the talent pool for everyone. And it is a leading indicator for how AI gets deployed when secrecy is non-negotiable: quant firms are building research agents that run locally so that alpha-generating ideas never pass through an external API. Any company with comparably sensitive intellectual property should expect to follow the same path.

Also seen as prop trading, high-frequency trading, proprietary trading firms, prop shops

Deeper dive

How it works. A trading system is a pipeline. Market data feeds arrive over dedicated links, a decision engine evaluates them against a model of expected price movement, and an order is sent to the exchange, with the whole loop engineered for Ultra-low latency so that the firm sees and acts before competitors. Engineering practices reflect that: languages such as C++ and increasingly Rust for predictable performance, custom hardware, kernel-level tuning and exhaustive testing, since a bug can lose money at machine speed. Research sits alongside, with quants mining historical data for alpha factors, signals with predictive power, and validating them before they reach production.

Why it matters now. Two shifts connect the sector to the AI story. First, the competitive frontier moved from speed to models, so prop firms have become sophisticated machine learning shops in their own right and a serious rival in AI lab talent competition, often paying top-of-market compensation for the same profile of engineer the labs want. Second, the firms are early adopters of agentic research under strict confidentiality. Recent work post-trains local research agents to explore alpha factors with deliberate diversity, explicitly to remove dependence on external APIs so that strategies never leave the firm's environment. That makes quant trading a test case for on-premise agents in any secrecy-bound domain.

Trade-offs. The culture that makes these firms strong is expensive and narrow. Extreme performance engineering is hard to hire for and slow to change, and the payoff from a model edge decays as competitors find the same signal, so research must run continuously. Local agents preserve confidentiality but forgo the frontier capability of hosted models and require the firm to run its own inference at scale. There is also a systemic concern: as more firms deploy similar model-driven agents, correlated behaviour across markets becomes more likely.

What to watch. Whether prop firms extend local agents from research into live decision-making, how their compensation offers shape lab hiring, and whether the local-agent pattern they are pioneering spreads to legal, pharmaceutical and defence work with similar secrecy requirements.
