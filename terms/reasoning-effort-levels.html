Reasoning effort levels

first seen 2026-W36 · 9 links · 3 min read

Reasoning effort levels let a customer decide how hard a model should think. At low effort the model answers quickly with little hidden reasoning; at max it may spend most of its output budget on internal deliberation before replying. The same prompt can therefore cost different amounts depending on the setting, with tests showing a 33-times cost range across five levels and two levels behaving identically.

For an executive the dial is one of the biggest levers on AI spend. Large deployments have standardised on medium effort as the best cost-to-output ratio, and labs themselves advise dropping the level when the extra work costs more than it returns. The setting also complicates vendor comparisons: one model at max can beat a rival at medium on a coding benchmark for a quarter of the cost, and medium can beat max on the same benchmark at a fifth of the cost, so headline scores mean little without the effort level attached. Procurement and engineering should agree default levels per workload.

Also seen as effort dial, thinking levels, low/medium/high/xhigh/max

Deeper dive

How it works. Effort levels control the amount of Inference-time compute a model spends on hidden reasoning before producing its visible answer. Internally this maps to a budget or a bias on how many Reasoning tokens the model generates, which the provider exposes as a small set of named levels rather than a raw token count. Research is moving toward models that make the choice themselves, learning when to think and when to answer directly based on the difficulty of the request, which is the model-internal counterpart of the user-set dial. Until that matures, the level is a parameter the caller sets per request.

Why it matters now. The dial has become central to comparing the newest models. Reviews of GPT-6 Luna and its sibling found that most of the difference in their figures came from effort settings rather than the models, with Luna at max beating the other at medium on a coding benchmark for a quarter of the cost, and separately medium beating max at a fifth of the cost. On Claude Opus 5.5 the max setting has twice consumed the entire output budget in reasoning and returned nothing, which led at least one careful tester to distrust it for longer work. Spreads of 13 points between low and extra high on a coding benchmark, and an 11-times usage range across levels, show that rankings depend on the setting. Enterprises such as Uber have defaulted to medium, and labs publish guidance to reduce effort when the extra thinking is not worth its cost, all of which ties effort to Cost per task and to Frontier model pricing.

Trade-offs. More effort is not monotonically better. Beyond a point extra reasoning adds latency and cost without accuracy, and at the extreme can exhaust the budget before an answer appears. Levels are also opaque: two settings may be functionally identical, and the mapping from level to tokens differs by vendor and by model version, so a default tuned for one release can misbehave on the next. Benchmark results without a stated effort level are close to meaningless, and vendors have an incentive to report at max while customers run at medium.

What to watch. Whether models learn to set their own effort reliably, whether benchmark reporting standardises on declared levels, and how pricing evolves if providers charge for reasoning tokens differently from output tokens.

Inference-time compute

Inference-time compute is computation spent when a model answers, through longer reasoning, multiple candidate answers, verification or tool calls, rather than during training.

Cost per task

Cost per task is the dollar cost of getting a unit of work actually completed by an AI system, measured at a given quality level, rather than the price per token or the score on a benchmark.

Reasoning tokens

Reasoning tokens are the internal deliberation a model generates before producing its visible answer, billed and counted like any other output.

Reasoning traces

Reasoning traces are the recorded step-by-step thinking a model produces on its way to an answer, whether shown to the user, hidden, or summarised.
