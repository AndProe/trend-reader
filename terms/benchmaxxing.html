Benchmaxxing

first seen 2026-W33 · 11 links · 3 min read

Benchmaxxing is the deliberate optimisation of a model for the public benchmarks that appear in launch tables. Labs know which test sets reviewers and buyers look at, and they shape training data and post-training so the model performs well on those sets. This is distinct from fraud: no one is falsifying numbers. It is closer to teaching to the exam, and every lab does some of it.

The consequence for a buyer is that a launch table measures preparation for the test more than fitness for the buyer's work. Chinese labs, which compete on headline scores to close a perceived gap, have especially strong incentives. Procurement and model-selection decisions should therefore lean on private evaluations built from the organisation's own tasks, and headline gains between releases should be discounted until confirmed on those.

Also seen as benchmark gaming, benchmark tuning

Deeper dive

How it works. A model's score on a public benchmark depends on how much of the training and post-training distribution resembles the test. Labs have staffed teams to collect data shaped like the popular test sets, tune reinforcement learning on environments that are easy to verify and close to the benchmark format, and choose which numbers to publish. None of this requires seeing the test items; it is optimisation against the benchmark's style. Contamination, where test items leak into training data, is the involuntary version of the same effect. The practice is distinguished from fraud because the scores are real; they simply measure a narrower thing than they appear to.

Why it matters now. The gap between headline scores and general capability widens as benchmarks approach saturation and as labs compete on release tables. One recurring critique is that Chinese labs, which market their releases on parity with the frontier, face the strongest incentives to tune for the public sets, and this feeds the debate about the real size of the Open-closed capability gap. Expert re-grading of physics evaluations found broken graders and near-saturation of leading suites, which cuts both ways: broken graders can understate as well as inflate. One capability index has been checked for overfitting by looking at how scores lag on newer items, and at least one model builder refuses public benchmarks entirely for this reason.

Trade-offs. Public Benchmarks remain useful because they are comparable across labs and cheap to run; abandoning them removes a shared yardstick. Counter-measures include anonymous or double-blind releases, which blunt the accusation that a lab tuned for a known reviewer, and benchmark designs that separate in-distribution rules from out-of-distribution ones so a shortcut learner scores differently from a rule learner. Generated rather than collected test items resist tuning but are harder to validate. The related phenomenon of Evaluation awareness, where a model recognises it is being tested, makes the problem harder to detect.

What to watch. Whether double-blind evaluation pilots spread; whether buyers shift procurement to Internal benchmarks built from their own workloads; and whether the Release cadence of frontier labs keeps benchmark tables as the primary marketing instrument or replaces them with task-level evidence.

Open-closed capability gap

The open-closed capability gap is the measured lag between the best openly downloadable model and the best closed frontier model, usually expressed as the number of months it takes open models to reach a given capability level.
