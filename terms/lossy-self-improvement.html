Lossy self-improvement

first seen 2026-W37 · 7 links · 3 min read

Lossy self-improvement is a counter-position to the idea of a self-reinforcing intelligence explosion. It accepts that models now do real research work, write code for their successors and speed up experiments. It denies that this closes a loop, because each gain is partly lost: the research that can be automated is a narrow slice, scaling laws demand exponentially more resources for each step, adding more parallel agents yields less each time, and the bottlenecks that remain are physical and political rather than intellectual.

For an executive the thesis offers a planning frame. If it holds, the next few years bring models that are superhuman at goal-directed tasks in mathematics and software but limited in intuition and creativity, with serving costs falling faster than capability rises. That favours investment in deploying current capability efficiently over waiting for a discontinuity, and it argues against strategies that assume a single lab pulls irreversibly ahead.

Also seen as lossy RSI

Deeper dive

What it claims. The thesis has three parts. First, the portion of AI research that agents can automate, largely experiment execution and code, is small relative to the whole, and the remainder is bounded by the exponential resource costs written into scaling laws. Second, deploying many agents in parallel on research shows diminishing returns, because coordination overhead and the scarcity of good ideas dominate once execution is cheap. Third, what gets built is governed by compute supply, capital and politics, none of which a smarter model changes quickly. The conclusion is that improvement is real but lossy: each turn of the loop returns less than it consumes.

Why it matters now. The debate over Recursive self-improvement moved from theory to observation once labs began using agents to accelerate their own work and publishing measurements of internal automation. The lossy position reads those measurements as showing acceleration without compounding. Its prediction is a jagged frontier: models already superhuman at goal-seeking in maths and software, as the recent mathematical results illustrate, while remaining limited in intuition, taste and open-ended creativity. On this model the biggest near-term gains are in serving efficiency, Inference-time compute and cost rather than in peak intelligence, which is consistent with the trend of cheaper models catching up on benchmarks.

Trade-offs. The thesis can be wrong in either direction. If Reinforcement learning environments become good enough to train intuition and creativity, the narrow-slice argument weakens. If parallel agents learn to coordinate, diminishing returns may flatten later than assumed. Critics also note that resource and political bottlenecks can be removed by the same capital the labs are attracting. Supporters reply that every prior forecast of compounding gains has run into these same losses and that the burden of proof sits with the explosion view, a point that feeds directly into the Singularity debate and into estimates of P(doom).

What to watch. Lab measurements of research automation over time, benchmarks that separate goal-seeking from creative tasks, and whether efficiency gains continue to outpace capability gains at the frontier.
