Project Glasswing

first seen 2026-W35 · 7 links · 3 min read

Project Glasswing is a gated access scheme. Instead of releasing every capability of its Mythos models to all customers, Anthropic reserves some, particularly those relevant to cybersecurity, for partners it has vetted. Visa is a named member. The stated logic is that capabilities powerful enough to find and exploit vulnerabilities should reach defenders first and be kept from the general public until safeguards mature.

For an executive the programme raises a practical question: is the company inside or outside the gate. Members gain early use of capabilities that have measurably increased their vulnerability disclosures, which suggests a real security advantage. Non-members face a frontier that is partly closed to them regardless of budget, and a market in which access, not price, becomes the differentiator. The programme is also a template that other labs may copy, so procurement and security teams should treat trusted-access status as something to pursue and to plan around.

Deeper dive

How it works. Glasswing sits within the broader class of Trusted-access programs. A lab identifies capabilities it considers too sensitive for open release, defines vetting criteria for partners, and provides those partners with access under contractual and technical controls. The withheld capabilities in this case belong to Mythos 5 and its successor Mythos 5.1, and centre on offensive and defensive cyber work such as vulnerability discovery. Partners are expected to use the capability for defence and to report what they find, which produces the measurable output that outside analysts have examined.

Why it matters now. The programme is the clearest instance of Access rationing, the practice of allocating frontier capability by trust rather than by price. Its effect is visible in the data: members show a statistically significant surge in vulnerability disclosures after joining, which is one of the few quantitative pieces of evidence in a debate otherwise dominated by claims. That evidence bears directly on AI cybersecurity skepticism, since sceptics argue that AI has not changed the security picture, while the disclosure surge indicates that gated tools are finding real flaws. Visa's participation, alongside its use of switchable open models inside its own harness, shows how large enterprises are pairing gated frontier access with an open-model fallback.

Trade-offs. Rationing by trust concentrates advantage among incumbents who can pass vetting, and it makes the lab a gatekeeper of who gets to defend themselves well. It also creates an asymmetric market in which security vendors and cloud providers outside the programme compete at a disadvantage. The vetting itself is opaque, and there is no external audit of whether withheld capabilities remain withheld. On the other side, unrestricted release of vulnerability-finding capability would arm attackers as fast as defenders, and the surge in disclosures demonstrates the capability is potent enough to justify caution. The programme is a bet that a curated defender advantage beats open symmetry.

What to watch. How the membership expands and on what criteria, whether other labs launch equivalent programmes, whether disclosure data continues to show an effect as more members join, and whether regulators treat trusted-access schemes as a model or as a competition concern.
