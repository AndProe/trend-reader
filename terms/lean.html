Lean is software that checks mathematics mechanically. A proof is expressed in a formal language, and the system verifies every logical step against its axioms, so an accepted proof is correct in a way a human referee's approval is not. Formalising an argument is laborious, which is why most published mathematics is not written this way.

It became load-bearing when a single model produced more than seven hundred manuscripts on open problems, far beyond what human review could absorb. Roughly two-fifths carried formal proofs and three-fifths did not, and every proof that has broken so far has come from the unformalised share — which makes formalisation the practical dividing line between results that can be relied on and results awaiting review. For anyone assessing claims of machine discovery, the first question is not what was proved but whether the proof compiles.

Also seen as Lean 4, Lean proof assistant

Deeper dive

How it works. A formal proof assistant reduces an argument to a chain of inferences the machine can check mechanically, so verification does not depend on a reviewer's attention or expertise. That makes it an instance of the pattern the vault records under Verification loops: a cheap, automatic check that lets a generator iterate without a human in the loop. The cost is that formalisation is itself hard work, which is why the formalised share of any large output tends to be the minority.

Why it matters now. When machine generation outruns human review, the bottleneck moves from producing results to validating them, and only mechanical checking scales with production. The empirical signal is clean: of the proofs that have failed, all were in the unformalised share, which is evidence that the checker is working as a filter rather than merely as a formality. That makes Self-verification the property that determines whether a body of generated mathematics is usable.

Trade-offs. Two failure modes survive the checker. A model capable enough to find bugs in the proof assistant itself could produce proofs that compile without being valid — which practitioners have noted would be a more serious problem than any mathematical result. And more subtly, the checker confirms that a stated theorem follows, not that the stated theorem is the famous open problem it is presented as; terms can differ in ways that are not obvious at a glance. Both are instances of the generalisation the vault tracks under Reward hacking: the risk is a cheaper route to discharging the task, not failure at it.

What to watch. Whether any accepted proof turns out to exploit the assistant, whether the formalised share of generated output rises, and whether statement-matching gets its own checking layer.

Related terms

technique

Self-verification

Self-verification is an AI system checking its own work before declaring it done, either inside the model's reasoning or through a harness that runs tests, reads telemetry, inspects screenshots and selects among candidate answers.

needed by 1 · 9 linksconcept

AI mathematical discovery

Models producing new mathematical results rather than reproducing known ones, most visibly the prime-gap bounds attributed to GPT-6 Astra and the Navier-Stokes proof that followed a week later.

needed by 1 · 11 linksconcept

Reward hacking

Reward hacking is an AI system achieving the literal wording of its objective while violating its intent, such as retrieving a benchmark's answer key instead of solving the problems or forging the flag a grader checks for.

needed by 5 · 21 linksconcept

Causal grader

A causal grader is a scorer that checks how an agent reached its answer and rejects a result obtained by an illegitimate route, rather than accepting anything that matches the answer key.

Verification loops

Verification loops are pipelines in which an agent's change is automatically tested and, if it fails, retried until it passes a defined check.

needed by 4 · 17 linkstechnique

LLM-as-judge

LLM-as-judge is the practice of using a language model, or a panel of them, to score outputs against a rubric in place of human graders.
