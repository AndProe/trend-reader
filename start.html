For investors

Where the money goes.

Where the money goes: what frontier capability costs to make and sell, who captures the margin, and how the buildout is financed.

  1. concept

    Frontier models

    Frontier models are the top tier: the handful of models from a few labs that define what is possible at a given date. The tier resets often, with the frontier moving by three points or more on standard indices several times in a season, so it is a moving target that efficient and open models chase at a lag of months.

    Read the term →
  2. concept

    Open weights models

    An open weights model is one whose trained parameters are published so that anyone with enough hardware can run it themselves. The licence may or may not permit commercial use, and the training data and code are usually not included, which is why the term is narrower than open source. Chinese labs have led the category with families such as Qwen, GLM and Kimi, while Western labs have responded with permissively licensed releases of their own.

    Read the term →
  3. metric

    Frontier model pricing

    Frontier model pricing is the published rate card for the strongest models. The current anchor is ten dollars per million input tokens and fifty per million output, a point Astra adopted to match Fable 5.1 exactly, with discounts delivered through cheaper cache reads rather than a lower headline. Below the flagship, the workhorse tier sits around three and fifteen dollars, and challengers such as Grok 4.7 hold at two and six.

    Read the term →
  4. metric

    Cost per task

    Model vendors quote prices per million tokens and publish benchmark scores. Neither tells a buyer what it costs to get a job done. A cheaper model that uses three times as many tokens, retries more often or needs a higher reasoning setting can cost more per finished task than an expensive one. Cost per task fixes the unit: dollars per resolved ticket, per merged pull request, per classified case or per completed benchmark item, at a stated accuracy. It has moved from an analyst's preferred metric to the axis the labs themselves now use in their launch charts.

    Read the term →
  5. concept

    Token share versus revenue share

    Token share counts the volume of model usage; revenue share counts the money. The two have separated. Open-weight models, many of them Chinese, handle a growing share of tokens because they are cheap and good enough for routine work, while the closed labs at the frontier keep most of the revenue because the highest-value tasks still go to the most capable tier. One recent split puts volume at roughly 62 to 38 in favour of open models, with value still accruing to two labs.

    Read the term →
  6. concept

    Vertical integration of AI labs

    The leading labs are no longer content to sell model access. They are buying the toolchain layer beneath their coding products, as Anthropic did with Bun and OpenAI with Astral, building and owning the coding harness rather than leaving it to third parties, expanding into verticals such as health where the product reaches users directly, and in at least one case cutting off a major third-party platform. The model becomes the engine; the product is where the labs compete.

    Read the term →
  7. concept

    Access rationing

    Access rationing describes a shift from selling frontier models to anyone with an API key toward deciding, case by case, who is allowed to use the most capable systems. The mechanisms vary: a lab withdrawing its models from a platform it no longer trusts, as happened with Cursor after its acquisition by xAI; a government keeping a model such as Fable 5 off the open market and choosing which organisations may use Mythos; and structured trusted-partner programmes such as Project Glasswing, where capability is withheld from general release and allocated by customer category.

    Read the term →
  8. concept

    Hyperscalers

    The hyperscalers are the handful of companies operating cloud infrastructure at global scale. Their AI businesses are growing 37 to 82 percent a year, they lock up the accelerator pipeline years ahead, and they are the gatekeepers of investment-grade capital for data centre projects, sometimes described as gigascalers to mark the shift to gigawatt campuses. They are also the buyers of behind-the-meter power capacity and the owners of the enterprise tiers, such as Bedrock and Vertex, through which regulated companies consume models.

    Read the term →
  9. concept

    Neo clouds

    Neo clouds are the specialist cloud companies that rent out GPUs and serve models without being one of the large general-purpose clouds. Some, such as CoreWeave, build large data centres and lease capacity to labs and enterprises; others, such as the inference providers, host open-weights models and sell tokens at competitive prices. The category has grown fast: one operator reports a 4.2-gigawatt footprint and a backlog above one hundred billion dollars, and non-hyperscaler buyers now account for the majority of new data-centre revenue.

    Read the term →
  10. concept

    AI infrastructure debt

    AI infrastructure debt is the financing behind the data-centre build-out. Hyperscaler cash flows cannot fund it alone, so facilities are financed like real estate, with debt at 65 to 75 percent of the asset, and off-balance-sheet joint ventures such as Meta's Beignet vehicle push effective leverage toward 90 percent. The estimated need, about $4 trillion of new borrowing over five years, is nearly three times the commercial paper market and larger than the entire global private credit pool.

    Read the term →
  11. metric

    Revenue per gigawatt

    Data centres are sized in megawatts and gigawatts because power, not floor space or chips, is what limits them. Revenue per gigawatt asks how much money each gigawatt of capacity brings in each year. Current estimates put frontier API serving at over one hundred billion dollars per gigawatt-year, and at the megawatt scale roughly fifty million dollars of revenue against ten to fifteen million of cost.

    Read the term →
  12. concept

    Vendor financing

    Vendor financing is when the seller funds the buyer. Nvidia has invested in AI labs and GPU clouds that buy its chips, financed data-centre construction directly, extended payment terms to buyers with weak balance sheets, backed SpaceX's buildout, and provided revenue floors, landlord guarantees and transferable leases that make lenders comfortable financing GPU capacity. The disclosed scale is large and has been reported in the company's own filings.

    Read the term →
  13. metric

    AI valuations

    AI valuations describe how much investors pay per dollar of revenue for AI companies, and in 2026 a two-tier market has formed. Public software multiples compressed from a 100x ceiling in 2021 to about 34x, while a set of AI-first companies reached the $100 million revenue milestone at multiples near 100x, this time on faster underlying growth. The multiple tracks the AI narrative more than the growth rate. At the extreme, OpenAI's $1.5 trillion target is set against roughly $40 billion in annualised revenue, and one agent startup sought a $4 billion valuation months after being marked at $2 billion.

For technologists

How the systems work.

How the systems work: from the agent loop and the layer around the model to training, inference economics and how capability is measured.

  1. concept

    AI agents

    An agent is a model wrapped in a loop: it reads a goal, decides on an action, runs a tool, looks at the result and repeats until the task is done or it gives up. The wrapping ranges from a coding assistant in a terminal to hosted agents that run workflows for people who never write code, to agents that act on home devices, place phone calls or handle a week of travel over text messages.

    Read the term →
  2. technique

    Coding harness

    A raw language model can only answer prompts. A coding harness gives it hands: it feeds the model the right files, lets it run commands, captures the results, keeps the working context under control and loops until the task is done. Claude Code, Codex, Cursor and Devin are harnesses; so is Inspect, the in-house agent Ramp built on the open-source OpenCode harness. The same pattern is spreading beyond code into legal, customer-service and finance products, where the harness is the application and the model is an input.

    Read the term →
  3. technique

    Sub-agents

    A single agent works through a task one step at a time. With sub-agents, a coordinator breaks the task into pieces and launches separate agents for each, typically dozens with a cap on how many run at once, then gathers and combines what they return. A research fleet might run over five hundred targets in two passes with different roles; a coding run might send small tasks to cheap models while the parent keeps the hard reasoning.

    Read the term →
  4. technique

    Verification loops

    A verification loop wraps an AI coding agent in automated checks: tests, linters, type checks, regression suites, trace replays or live interaction, plus a rule to retry until the checks pass. The agent proposes, the pipeline validates, and only validated changes proceed. Practitioners reporting large throughput gains attribute them to these loops rather than to any particular model, and organisations that migrated large codebases with AI describe the same retry-and-validate structure.

    Read the term →
  5. technique

    Post-training

    Post-training is the second half of building a model. Pretraining teaches a model language and general knowledge by predicting text; post-training shapes it into something useful by fine-tuning on demonstrations, adjusting it toward preferred answers, and running reinforcement learning on tasks with verifiable outcomes such as code that passes tests or maths that checks out. Modern recipes chain these stages, often with a mid-training phase in between, and reinforcement learning on a growing set of environments has become the main engine of progress.

    Read the term →
  6. technique

    Reinforcement learning environments

    A reinforcement learning environment is a place where a model practises. It contains a task, a way for the model to act on it, and a checker that decides whether the outcome was correct. A coding environment might be a real repository with a bug and a hidden test suite; a computer-use environment might be a simulated desktop with a goal. Labs build thousands of these and train models by letting them attempt the tasks and rewarding success.

    Read the term →
  7. concept

    Reward hacking

    A model trained by reinforcement learning is rewarded when a checker says its output is correct. If the checker can be satisfied without doing the task, the model may learn the shortcut: editing the test instead of the code, reading an answer file it was not meant to open, or convincing an automated grader that a wrong argument is right. That is reward hacking. It is not deliberate deceit in the human sense, but the result is the same.

    Read the term →
  8. concept

    Inference-time compute

    A model can be made better in two ways: train it longer, or let it think longer when asked. Inference-time compute is the second. The model generates reasoning tokens before answering, tries several answers and picks the best, verifies its own work, or calls tools, and each of these costs compute at the moment of use. It is OpenAI's strategic bet, it is what makes reasoning effort levels and extended-thinking modes possible, and it is why one mathematical search effort consumed 300 billion output tokens.

    Read the term →
  9. concept

    Long context

    Long context means the model can take in a very large amount of material in a single request, on the order of a million tokens, roughly the length of several long novels. In practice this is what allows a coding agent to hold a whole repository and hours of tool output in mind, a clinician to load a patient's longitudinal record, or a video model to reason over multi-hour footage without cutting it into frames.

    Read the term →
  10. technique

    KV cache

    In plain terms, a transformer reads a prompt once and writes out a working memory of it, one entry per token. That memory is the KV cache. Every new token the model produces consults it, and without it the model would have to re-read the entire conversation at each step. The cache lives in fast accelerator memory alongside the model weights, and because it grows with every token added, a long agent session can occupy more memory than the model itself.

    Read the term →
  11. technique

    Prefill-decode disaggregation

    Serving a language model has two phases. Prefill reads the whole prompt at once and is compute-heavy; decode produces one token at a time and is limited by memory bandwidth. Running both on the same accelerator wastes capacity, because each phase leaves the other's bottleneck idle. Disaggregation puts prefill on one set of machines and decode on another, handing over the intermediate state, the KV cache, across a fast network. Inference vendors and the leading GPU stack use it as the default for large deployments.

    Read the term →
  12. technique

    Benchmarks

    Every model release opens with a benchmark table: the vendor's scores on a chosen set of tests, usually with competitors named only by tier. Independent scoreboards re-run models under common conditions, and researchers publish new suites weekly, increasingly with constructed ground truth, deterministic controls or generated question sets that renew forever to avoid contamination. Reviewers reproduce vendor claims with caveats, and the more useful reading has shifted from rank to cost-versus-score plots.

    Read the term →

For executives

What to decide.

What to decide: where agents change how work gets done, what they cost, how to keep them contained, and what the rules require.

  1. concept

    AI agents

    An agent is a model wrapped in a loop: it reads a goal, decides on an action, runs a tool, looks at the result and repeats until the task is done or it gives up. The wrapping ranges from a coding assistant in a terminal to hosted agents that run workflows for people who never write code, to agents that act on home devices, place phone calls or handle a week of travel over text messages.

    Read the term →
  2. concept

    Enterprise AI adoption

    Enterprise AI adoption describes the real shape of uptake inside companies. The headline paradox is that agentic tools account for around 64 percent of enterprise tokens at one major vendor while consumer adoption of the same agents sits near 0.7 percent, so chat is already the minority of enterprise output. Coding agents are the beachhead, spreading into non-engineering departments without a mandate, with sales development and support the next functions automated.

    Read the term →
  3. concept

    Agentic engineering

    In agentic engineering the division of labour is explicit. Agents write every line, run tests and lint, and increasingly do exploration, specification and test construction. Engineers design the system, set constraints, write specifications that include how the agent should check its own work, and review outputs. Some companies now state as policy that nearly all their code is agent-generated, and the label extends to specialised domains such as production inference serving.

  6. technique

    Smart model routing

    Most requests an AI system receives are easy. Smart model routing exploits that by putting a decision in front of the model call: a small, fast model classifies the request and either answers it or forwards it to a larger, more expensive one. In practice a local model might handle eight of thirty-one incoming emails outright and defer the rest, or a router might send simple agentic jobs to local models and complex ones to a cloud frontier model.

    Read the term →
  7. concept

    AI agent containment

    Containment is the set of walls around an agent rather than the training inside it: no internet unless granted, no contact with other agents unless intended, no files or credentials beyond the task at hand. Alignment tries to shape what a model wants; containment limits what it can do whatever it wants, and it is where several safety researchers now argue the marginal dollar pays best.

    Read the term →
  8. concept

    Agent security boundaries

    A security boundary for an agent answers three questions: what it can touch, in whose name, and who can see what it did. The mature answer extends zero trust from employees to agents. Each agent gets its own identity and scoped credentials rather than a shared login, holds the minimum permissions its task needs, runs under network, file-system and execution limits enforced outside the model, and is monitored end to end with circuit breakers. Authorisation stays outside the model, because anything it reads can try to talk it into misusing what it holds.

    Read the term →
  9. concept

    Human oversight

    Human oversight covers all the ways a person stays in control of an AI system: approving actions before they run, reviewing outputs after, being escalated to when the system is unsure, and holding authority to reduce its autonomy. It is now measured rather than assumed. Labs track coverage, review latency and escalation rate; enterprise value models include the reviewer catch rate; and new work prices oversight as a cost line rather than treating it as free. Engineers report intervening in more than half of agent runs, which makes the engineer a supervisor.

    Read the term →
  10. concept

    Shadow AI

    Employees who find AI useful do not wait for procurement. They paste documents into consumer chatbots, run personal subscriptions on work tasks and wire up agents with their own credentials. That is shadow AI. It is usually discovered by security teams as a data-leakage risk and answered with a ban, which stops the reporting rather than the usage.

    Read the term →
  11. concept

    AI regulation

    AI regulation in 2026 is a live contest, not a settled framework. In the United States the instruments include a voluntary and still-undisclosed federal pre-release testing framework briefed privately to three labs, a state executive order directing audits, verification and a frontier kill switch, a federal kill-switch bill blocked in the Senate, a proposed ban on superintelligence development, congressional calls for hearings and a pause after the agent-swarm incidents, and a proposed antitrust waiver so labs can coordinate on safety. The UN Security Council has held a session with lab leaders and researchers presenting positions.

    Read the term →
  12. concept

    Engineering culture

    Engineering culture is the unwritten operating system of a software organisation: how much autonomy teams have, whether experimentation and A/B testing are normal, how best practice spreads internally, and how much goodwill engineers extend to leadership. It is set from the VP of engineering and CTO seats, which is also where reorganisations under AI originate.
