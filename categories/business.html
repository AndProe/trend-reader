Business & economics

The economics of building and selling AI: pricing, margins, financing and how it is valued.

  1. metric

    Frontier model pricing

    Frontier model pricing is the published rate card for the strongest models. The current anchor is ten dollars per million input tokens and fifty per million output, a point Astra adopted to match Fable 5.1 exactly, with discounts delivered through cheaper cache reads rather than a lower headline. Below the flagship, the workhorse tier sits around three and fifteen dollars, and challengers such as Grok 4.7 hold at two and six.

  2. concept

    Enterprise AI adoption

    Enterprise AI adoption describes the real shape of uptake inside companies. The headline paradox is that agentic tools account for around 64 percent of enterprise tokens at one major vendor while consumer adoption of the same agents sits near 0.7 percent, so chat is already the minority of enterprise output. Coding agents are the beachhead, spreading into non-engineering departments without a mandate, with sales development and support the next functions automated.

    Read the term →
  3. concept

    AI infrastructure debt

    AI infrastructure debt is the financing behind the data-centre build-out. Hyperscaler cash flows cannot fund it alone, so facilities are financed like real estate, with debt at 65 to 75 percent of the asset, and off-balance-sheet joint ventures such as Meta's Beignet vehicle push effective leverage toward 90 percent. The estimated need, about $4 trillion of new borrowing over five years, is nearly three times the commercial paper market and larger than the entire global private credit pool.

  4. concept

    Bullwhip effect

    The bullwhip effect describes how a modest change in demand at the customer end becomes a large, lurching change further up the supply chain. Each tier orders ahead of what it actually needs so that it is not caught short, and those safety margins compound. In AI infrastructure the demand shock started with GPUs and has since moved through high-bandwidth memory into server CPUs, hard drives and data-centre shells, with networking gear the only major component not yet in shortage.

    Read the term →
  5. concept

    Release cadence

    Frontier models once arrived a year or more apart. In the agentic era the leading duopoly has been shipping a frontier release roughly every fifty-one days, one lab put out three Flash-class releases in six weeks, and a single week in September 2026 saw four labs ship updates at once. Chinese labs compete primarily on this axis, turning around releases in days where Western labs take months.

    Read the term →
  6. concept

    Compute as currency

    Open-weight models have made frontier-adjacent capability available to anyone, which invites the conclusion that everyone gets superintelligence and no one has an edge. The compute-as-currency view rejects that. If models are interchangeable, the constraint moves to what runs them: GPUs, data centres and electricity. Whoever holds compute holds the advantage, and compute behaves less like a utility bought by the hour and more like money, something to be traded, lent and priced against future returns.

  7. concept

    Token share versus revenue share

    Token share counts the volume of model usage; revenue share counts the money. The two have separated. Open-weight models, many of them Chinese, handle a growing share of tokens because they are cheap and good enough for routine work, while the closed labs at the frontier keep most of the revenue because the highest-value tasks still go to the most capable tier. One recent split puts volume at roughly 62 to 38 in favour of open models, with value still accruing to two labs.

  8. concept

    Vendor financing

    Vendor financing is when the seller funds the buyer. Nvidia has invested in AI labs and GPU clouds that buy its chips, financed data-centre construction directly, extended payment terms to buyers with weak balance sheets, backed SpaceX's buildout, and provided revenue floors, landlord guarantees and transferable leases that make lenders comfortable financing GPU capacity. The disclosed scale is large and has been reported in the company's own filings.

  9. metric

    Revenue per gigawatt

    Data centres are sized in megawatts and gigawatts because power, not floor space or chips, is what limits them. Revenue per gigawatt asks how much money each gigawatt of capacity brings in each year. Current estimates put frontier API serving at over one hundred billion dollars per gigawatt-year, and at the megawatt scale roughly fifty million dollars of revenue against ten to fifteen million of cost.

  10. concept

    Capability overhang

    Capability overhang names a simple observation: the models organisations already pay for can do far more than those organisations use them for. Most workflows, staff skills and procurement rules were designed for models two or three generations old, so each new release widens the gap rather than closing it. A slowdown in model progress would not erase the overhang; it would take years of adoption work to catch up with what is already available.

  11. metric

    Enterprise agent tokens

    Enterprise agent tokens measures where a vendor's enterprise customers spend their model usage: on conversational chat or on agents that execute multi-step work. OpenAI's reported figure is that 64 percent of enterprise tokens went to its Codex agent rather than to ChatGPT by June 2026. Set against near-zero consumer adoption of the same agent, around 0.7 percent, it is the central paradox of the current market.

    Read the term →
  12. concept

    Lab consolidation

    Lab consolidation describes two linked expectations. The first is that only a few laboratories can afford to train frontier models, so the field will narrow to those few. The second is that enterprise buyers, as they did with cloud, will settle on one or two vendors for each workload once every serious supplier clears a quality bar, at which point price and integration decide.

  13. concept

    Vertical integration of AI labs

    The leading labs are no longer content to sell model access. They are buying the toolchain layer beneath their coding products, as Anthropic did with Bun and OpenAI with Astral, building and owning the coding harness rather than leaving it to third parties, expanding into verticals such as health where the product reaches users directly, and in at least one case cutting off a major third-party platform. The model becomes the engine; the product is where the labs compete.

    Read the term →
  14. concept

    Data flywheel

    A data flywheel is a self-reinforcing loop: people use a model, their prompts, corrections and task outcomes become training signal, the model improves, and the improvement draws more use. The concept is distinct from a taste flywheel, which runs on human judgement about what good output looks like rather than on raw volume.

    Read the term →
  15. concept

    Commoditise your complement

    Every product has complements, the things customers buy alongside it. If the complement gets cheaper, demand for your product rises, so a company with a strong position in one layer has an incentive to drive the price of the neighbouring layer toward zero. In AI the model itself has become the complement that several large players want commoditised. Nvidia releases open models and training data because cheap, abundant models sell more GPUs. Meta pushes tokens into the market at prices that hurt companies whose business is selling tokens. OpenAI's leadership describes a version of the same logic from the middle: partners above it commoditise the model, while OpenAI works to commoditise the chip below it.

  16. event

    Anthropic IPO

    Anthropic has been reported since late August 2026 to be preparing a public offering that could match the largest on record, with a filing possible as soon as that month. Later reporting framed the lab's very large compute build-out as pre-IPO positioning and described the process as on track despite safety concerns. The lab's leadership continues to call its own product category an existential risk while preparing to sell shares in it, which is the tension commentators focus on.

  17. concept

    Platform risk

    Platform risk describes what happens when a business is built on someone else's platform and the platform owner changes the rules. In AI the specific form is dependence on one lab's closed models. The lab sets prices, deprecates versions and, unless contracts say otherwise, may retain de-identified usage data that improves its own models. If the lab then launches a product in the customer's market, it competes with insight drawn from that customer's traffic.

  18. concept

    Two-tier data pricing

    Two-tier data pricing offers one model under two contracts. On the standard tier the customer pays full price and the vendor never trains on their traffic. On the contributor tier the same model costs roughly 92 to 95 percent less, and in exchange the customer's prompts and outputs become training data. Meta introduced the pattern with Muse Spark 1.3, priced at $1.25 and $4.25 per million input and output tokens on the standard tier against $0.10 and $0.20 on the contributor tier.

  19. metric

    AI revenue requirement

    The AI revenue requirement is a back-of-the-envelope figure with large consequences. Start from the borrowing needed to build AI data centres, roughly $4 trillion over five years, apply the interest and depreciation such assets carry, and the industry must earn on the order of $1.2 to $1.5 trillion a year to service them. A separate published estimate puts the figure at $2.5 trillion. Current AI revenue across all vendors is a small fraction of either number.

  20. concept

    Humanoid robot economics

    A humanoid robot's cost is driven less by motors and more by its brain. The planning and vision models need accelerator-class compute, which can either ride on the robot or run in a data centre and stream instructions over a network. Analysis of total cost of ownership compares an onboard Jetson Thor with an offloaded B300 and finds that shared data centre GPUs cost less per unit of compute once about five robots share them, because a home robot is only active 4 to 8 percent of the time. Meanwhile Chinese makers claim aggressive deployment and unit prices, backed by industrial policy, while the training data needed for dexterity is expensive to collect.

  21. metric

    AI valuations

    AI valuations describe how much investors pay per dollar of revenue for AI companies, and in 2026 a two-tier market has formed. Public software multiples compressed from a 100x ceiling in 2021 to about 34x, while a set of AI-first companies reached the $100 million revenue milestone at multiples near 100x, this time on faster underlying growth. The multiple tracks the AI narrative more than the growth rate. At the extreme, OpenAI's $1.5 trillion target is set against roughly $40 billion in annualised revenue, and one agent startup sought a $4 billion valuation months after being marked at $2 billion.

    Read the term →
  22. metric

    Gross margin

    Gross margin measures what is left of a dollar of revenue after paying the direct cost of delivering the product. For a chipmaker that means wafers and packaging; for a model provider it means the compute that serves inference, before training costs and distribution revenue share. The reported numbers vary widely: Nvidia runs near 75 percent, estimates for frontier inference range from 60 to 85 percent, and one lab's margin has been tracked from minus 94 percent to 40 to 50 percent as scale and efficiency improved.

  23. concept

    Cache pricing

    When an application sends a model the same long instructions, documents or conversation history on every call, the provider can keep the processed form of that text in memory and charge much less to reuse it. Cache pricing is that reduced rate. With the Fable 5.1 release Anthropic cut cache reads by 75 percent to $0.25 per million tokens while leaving its headline per-token prices unchanged, and a later 60 percent cut applied to Opus 5.5 cache reads. The claimed 25 to 45 percent saving for customers sits almost entirely in this line of the price sheet.

    Read the term →
  24. concept

    AI-native companies

    AI-native companies are organisations designed on the assumption that agents do most of the production work. In practice this means very small, talent-dense teams, often startups founded in 2023 or later that used AI from the first commit, and a set of AI-first vertical companies in law, customer service and similar fields that reached significant revenue with headcounts a fraction of the previous generation's.

  25. concept

    Price elasticity

    Price elasticity measures how sensitive customers are to price. If a small price cut produces a large jump in usage, demand is elastic; if buyers keep paying whatever a product costs, it is inelastic. In AI the evidence points to a specific shape: corporate spending data shows that buyers do not pay a premium proportional to how capable a model is. A model that is twice as good on benchmarks does not command twice the price, because most tasks have fixed requirements and any model that clears the bar is good enough.

  26. concept

    Complementary assets

    When a technology is easy to copy or licence, the economics of who profits from it depend less on who invented it than on who controls the things it needs to be useful. Those things are the complementary assets. For AI they are compute capacity, proprietary data, distribution channels to customers and, in the enterprise, verified operational knowledge of how a business actually works. A model is rented and getting cheaper; the assets around it are scarce and getting more valuable.

  27. concept

    Neolabs

    A neolab is a new laboratory formed around a small group of well-known researchers with a very large seed round and no product. Examples in the vault include Discovery Loop, Ineffable Intelligence, SSI, Thinking Machines, Motif and Recursive, the last founded by senior researchers with a multibillion-dollar seed. They do not build data centres; they rent GPUs from hyperscalers or neoclouds, which makes them among the most valuable customers those providers have.

  28. event

    Cursor-xAI acquisition

    In April 2026 SpaceX and xAI acquired Cursor, one of the most widely used AI coding editors, for about $60 billion. The logic was an exchange of assets: Cursor had a large stream of real coding interactions and telemetry that xAI lacked for training, and xAI had GPU capacity sitting idle that Cursor's workload could fill. The deal was reportedly driven by direct concern at the top of xAI that Grok was falling behind the other frontier models.

  29. concept

    Product-led growth

    Product-led growth, or PLG, means letting customers try, adopt and pay for software on their own. Instead of a sales team qualifying leads and running demos, the product offers a free tier, guides the user to value quickly, and converts them to paid plans as usage grows. The model works when onboarding is smooth enough that users succeed without help, which historically limited it to simple tools.

  30. metric

    SpaceX AI revenue

    SpaceX has said its AI revenue could exceed its launch and satellite business by the fourth quarter of 2026. The path modelled for it is capacity: ten gigawatts of compute by 2027, sold at thirty to fifty million dollars per megawatt-year, which works out to roughly three hundred billion dollars of annual recurring revenue, with Microsoft projected as the largest buyer. The claim rests on building power and data-centre capacity faster than incumbents, including in orbit.

    Read the term →
  31. concept

    Ads in AI

    Advertising in AI assistants means sponsored placements, recommendations or answers inside a conversational product, sold to advertisers in the way search advertising is sold today. OpenAI's ad business is reported to have reached a $1 billion run rate, which is the first evidence that consumer assistants can monetise attention at scale rather than relying only on paid tiers. Reader sentiment polls in the same period were mixed, with a substantial share of users saying they dislike the idea.
